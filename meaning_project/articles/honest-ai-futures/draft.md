# How to Honestly Talk About the Future of AI

### A three-layer framework — and why most predictions fail before they begin

---

A few weeks ago I ran a study I was sure would work.

The idea was clean. When ChatGPT arrived in late 2022, Stack Overflow — the place programmers
go to ask and answer questions — started bleeding activity. Lots of people have measured *how
much* it bled. I wanted to ask a more human question: did the people who left differ from the
people who stayed in *why* they had been there? Some contributors answer questions to build
mastery. Some do it for reputation and status. Some do it for the community. My bet was that
when AI makes good answers instant and free, the status-seekers — whose whole currency just got
devalued — would walk first, while the community-minded would hang on.

I pre-registered the prediction. I froze the definitions before I looked at the outcomes, so I
couldn't fudge them later. Then I pulled the data on 50,305 contributors and ran the analysis.

The status-seekers were the *most* likely to stay. The exact opposite of my prediction.

There was a second hypothesis — that community-minded contributors, instead of leaving, would
"migrate" into commenting and editing roles. That one looked beautiful: a strong, clean effect.
Then I ran the placebo test — the same analysis on a fake shock one year *earlier*, before
ChatGPT existed. The beautiful effect showed up there too, almost identically. Which meant it
had nothing to do with AI. It was just who those people always were.

Two hypotheses, both dead. By the usual standards of "did I find something exciting," the
project failed.

And yet I'd trust that null result more than almost anything I read about AI and the future on
any given day. Here's why — and it's the reason I'm starting this whole project.

## The discourse isn't dumb. It's blurred.

Open any feed and you'll find confident claims about what AI will do to work and society. *Half
of all jobs gone by 2030. UBI will fix it. UBI will bankrupt us. This is the printing press all
over again. This is nothing like anything before. We'll all find new work. There will be no new
work.*

It's tempting to call this noise, or stupidity. It's neither. Most of the people saying these
things are smart and sincere. The problem is subtler and more fixable: **they're wearing three
completely different kinds of claim as if they were the same garment.**

A statement about the future of AI can be:

- **🟢 Something we actually know** — backed by data, and in principle falsifiable.
- **🟡 Something we can reasonably infer** — by analogy and mechanism, but not prove.
- **🔴 Something we have to choose** — a value, a design, a bet about what we *should* do.

These three demand completely different standards of evidence. The single most common failure
in futurism — and the thing I want this project to refuse — is to take a 🔴 or a 🟡 and dress
it up as a 🟢. To say "the data shows we must…" when the data shows no such thing, and the
"must" was a value all along.

Let me make the three layers concrete, because once you see them you can't unsee them.

## 🟢 Layer one: what we actually know

This is the smallest layer, and the most precious. It's where my Stack Overflow study lives —
and where its limits live too.

The honest standard here is the standard of real empirical work: state your claim before you
see the answer, build in a way for the data to prove you wrong, and report what you find even
when it's boring or embarrassing. My status-seeker hypothesis was wrong and I'll tell you it
was wrong. That placebo test could have quietly stayed in a drawer; instead it killed my best
result, and that's exactly why the framework demands it.

A real example with bigger stakes: **does giving people money restore their sense of meaning?**
We have actual evidence, not vibes. Finland ran a basic-income experiment (2017–2018) and found
recipients reported better mental wellbeing and a stronger sense of economic security — but
employment effects were small, and the gains were about *relief and security*, not a
transformation of *purpose*. A large privately-funded US study giving people \$1,000 a month
for three years reported decidedly mixed results: real financial breathing room, without the
broader life transformation some had hoped for. Across the cash-transfer literature, the
pattern is stubborn enough to state plainly: **money reliably buys relief from material stress;
it does not reliably buy meaning.**

(Full disclosure, and exactly in the spirit of this piece: I wanted to tell you the US study
found its early wellbeing benefits *faded* by years two and three. I had it in my notes. But
when I went to verify it against the source before publishing, I couldn't confirm that specific
claim — so I've left it out. "Mixed results" is what I can stand behind. This is the framework
in action: if I can't show you the green, I don't get to paint it green.)

Notice how narrow that 🟢 claim is. It's not "UBI will save us" or "UBI won't work." It's a
specific, evidenced, bounded finding. That narrowness is the point. The green layer is small on
purpose — most of what we want to say about the future simply isn't in it, and pretending
otherwise is the original sin.

## 🟡 Layer two: what we can reasonably infer

Here we leave the safety of data, but we don't fall into pure speculation. We reason from
history and mechanism — *carefully*, and with the breakpoints stated out loud.

The big one: **is AI like the Industrial Revolution?** People invoke it constantly, in both
directions. ("Machines destroyed jobs and we ended up richer than ever" vs. "this time the
machine comes for the mind.") Both are doing 🟡 reasoning. The skill is not to pick a side but
to name *where the analogy holds and where it breaks*.

It holds at the level of structure: automate some kind of labour, old roles vanish, there's a
painful adjustment, eventually a new equilibrium — and how that equilibrium turns out depends
enormously on institutions that historically arrived decades late (unions, public education,
the welfare state).

But it may break on three specific variables — and naming them is more useful than any
confident verdict, because each is something we can actually watch:

1. **What gets automated.** The Industrial Revolution took muscle; humans retreated to
   cognition. Computers took routine cognition; humans retreated to judgment and creativity.
   AI aims at judgment and creativity themselves. The historical pattern was a *retreat to
   higher ground* — and the open question is whether there's higher ground left.
2. **Speed.** The Industrial Revolution unfolded across generations: parents were wrecked, but
   their children grew up inside the new structure. Re-training and new institutions have their
   own slow clock. If the shock moves faster than society can adjust, the reassuring "in the
   long run it worked out" never gets a chance to start.
3. **The hidden assumption in "new jobs will appear."** They always have — but only because
   when new work appeared, humans were better at it than machines. That precondition is exactly
   what's now in question. If AI can also do the new jobs, the mechanism that rescued us every
   previous time quietly stops working.

That's an honest 🟡 analysis. It doesn't tell you what *will* happen. It hands you a dashboard
of three dials to keep your eye on. That's the most a yellow claim can honestly give you — and
it's a lot.

## 🔴 Layer three: what we have to choose

Some questions aren't about prediction at all. *What kind of society do we want if most people
no longer need to work? What's owed to those displaced? Where should meaning come from when it
no longer comes from a job?*

No amount of data settles these. They're value choices and design problems. And the honest
standard here isn't "is it true" — it's **robustness**: not "this future will happen, so
prepare for it," but "which arrangements hold up well across a *wide range* of futures, because
we genuinely don't know which one we'll get?"

This is a real discipline, not hand-waving — it has a name (robust decision-making) and a track
record in fields that plan under deep uncertainty. The move is to stop pretending you can
forecast the one true future, and instead ask which bets you won't regret across many of them.

The cardinal sin lives right at the border of this layer. It's the policy advocate who says
"the evidence shows we must adopt X" — laundering a 🔴 value through a borrowed 🟢 lab coat.
Often the evidence is real but only supports a narrow 🟢 claim, while the "must" smuggles in a
choice the speaker never owned up to making. You're allowed to argue for X. You're not allowed
to pretend the data made the choice for you.

## Why this is freeing, not paralysing

You might think all this caution leads somewhere timid — *we can't know anything, so shrug.*
The opposite is true.

The framework doesn't forbid you from talking about the big, scary, important questions. It
lets you talk about them *honestly* — by making each sentence wear its true badge. You can
write a bold paragraph about post-work meaning, as long as the reader can tell which parts are
🟢 evidence, which are 🟡 inference, and which are 🔴 your values. Confidence and honesty stop
being enemies.

There's precedent for taking the unprovable seriously without faking certainty. The research on
"superforecasters" showed that the people who predict best aren't the boldest pundits — they're
the ones who think in calibrated probabilities, update constantly, and distinguish what they
know from what they're guessing. Entire fields plan rigorously for futures they can't predict.
Seriousness and humility are not opposites. The pundits who sound most certain are usually the
ones blurring the layers.

## The rule I'm holding myself to

So that's the standard for everything I'm going to write here, as I dig into what AI might do to
human work and meaning, and what — in policy, culture, and our own psychology — we can prepare.

Three layers, never blurred. When I show you data, I'll tell you exactly what it does and
doesn't show. When I reason by analogy, I'll point at where the analogy breaks. When I argue for
something, I'll own it as a value, not disguise it as a fact. And when I'm wrong — like I was,
twice, about those Stack Overflow contributors — I'll tell you that too.

In a conversation this important and this crowded, calibrated honesty is the rarest thing on
offer. It's the only thing I have worth giving. Hold me to it.
