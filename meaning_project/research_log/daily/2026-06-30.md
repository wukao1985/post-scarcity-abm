## Watchlist movement

- **New to log (outside window — July 2025):** Dominski & Lee, *"Advancing AI Capabilities
  and Evolving Labor Outcomes"* (Jacob Dominski, Yong Suk Lee [Notre Dame / Meta Generative
  AI & Upskilling Workshop / Wharton AI and the Future of Work]; arXiv:2507.08244, Jul 2025;
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.08244).
  🟢 **with significant caveats** (CPS-linked analysis; dynamic AI exposure scores derived
  from ChatGPT-4o + Claude 3.5 Sonnet task evaluations across a five-stage capability
  framework from ML→agentic AI; **exposure scores are model-derived, not independent, and
  two-provider rather than one**; working paper, not peer-reviewed; CPS is a survey, not
  admin data; the dynamic framing means the "shock" variable changes each month as AI
  benchmarks improve, which is methodologically creative but raises new endogeneity
  concerns not present in static-exposure designs).
  *What it actually shows:* Increases in the dynamic Occupational AI Exposure Score (as AI
  capability benchmarks improve) are associated with **decreases in total employment,
  increases in unemployment rates, and decreases in hours worked** in those occupations,
  using CPS microdata. Effects are more pronounced among **older and younger workers, men,
  and college-educated individuals**; occupations relying heavily on complex reasoning and
  problem-solving show the largest declines in full-time work.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* This paper is notable primarily because it
  **runs counter to the macro-null cluster** — the convergence of Humlum & Vestergaard
  (Denmark admin, precise null), Audoly et al. (NY Fed postings, pre-ChatGPT divergence),
  Massenkoff & McCrory (stock null, flow signal), and our own contract-buffer null — that
  constitute the project's current macro read ("aggregate employment intact, structural
  signals underneath"). Dominski & Lee find actual employment *stock* effects. The key
  unresolved question is methodological: does the dynamic exposure score pick up a real
  AI-employment effect, or does it proxy for some secular trend toward capability-intensive
  roles? The demographic concentration in **older + younger workers and college-educated
  men** overlaps but differs from the Stanford/ADP young-only signal. If the finding
  survives replication and peer review, it would meaningfully upgrade the "too early to
  see stock effects" reading in the evidence base. *Not yet an evidence-base candidate*
  (working paper, dynamic-score caveats, counterpoint to peer-reviewed findings pending
  resolution); flag for peer-review tracking and check against BLS tomorrow.
