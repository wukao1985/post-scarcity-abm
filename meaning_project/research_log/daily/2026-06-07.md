## Items

- **"Lonely Work: Gig Work, Workplace Technology and Working-Class Loneliness"** — Chris
  Bousquet; *Journal of Business Ethics* (Springer Nature), published March 16, 2026.
  https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10551-026-06289-6
  🔴 (normative ethics argument for regulatory reform) / 🟡 (empirically-informed — draws
  on gig economy isolation data, Jahoda's latent-functions framework, algorithmic management
  research; does not present original empirical data).
  *What it actually shows:* A philosophical / normative argument that three interconnected
  workplace technology trends have already "stripped away essential social goods" from
  low-income work at scale: (1) rise of platform/gig work eliminating stable co-worker
  relationships; (2) automation tools removing the human-collaborative tasks that generated
  social contact; (3) employee efficiency-monitoring technologies that squeeze out informal
  socialisation during the workday and increase scheduling unpredictability, making it
  harder to maintain outside relationships. The result is what Bousquet calls the
  *desocialization* of working-class work — a structural erosion of the Jahodian latent
  functions (social contact, collective purpose, time structure) that falls disproportionately
  on low-income workers who cannot exit to more autonomous work arrangements. The paper
  argues this is a distributive-justice issue that should motivate regulatory responses
  (portable benefits, scheduling predictability, right to disconnect, anti-monitoring rules),
  not merely an individual wellbeing concern. *Limits (state honestly):* this is a normative
  argument in an ethics journal — it does not present original data, and the empirical base
  it draws on is a selective synthesis rather than a systematic review; the causal chain from
  technology adoption to loneliness to harm rests on correlational and cross-sectional gig
  economy studies; the regulatory prescriptions are contested; the paper focuses on low-income
  gig workers, not knowledge workers or the broader population. *Why it matters:* the project
  has used Jahoda's latent functions as a foundational 🟢 anchor since the start. Bousquet
  provides the explicit argument that the *desocialization* of work via gig/automation
  technology is already happening at scale — not as a future post-labour scenario but as a
  present condition for low-wage workers. This is a useful 🔴 framework for the writing:
  the post-work meaning crisis is not purely a future risk; it has a present-tense version
  that is already heavily documented. It also sharpens the project's class dimension — the
  latent-functions loss lands first and hardest on workers who lack the option to exit platform
  relationships, not on professional workers who retain autonomy.
  → idea: "Two-speed desocialization" — piece arguing that the post-work meaning problem has
  already arrived for gig/low-wage workers (desocialization via technology, not job loss)
  while professional workers debate it as a future scenario; the policy question is partly
  about the speed differential, not only the end-state.
