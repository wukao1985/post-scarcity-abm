## Items

- **"Are We Automating the Joy Out of Work? Designing AI to Augment Work, Not Meaning"** —
  Ranjit, Zhou, Swayamditta & Quercia; arXiv:2603.14963 (submitted Mar 2026); presented CHI
  2026 (April, Barcelona). https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.14963
  🟢 (survey-based descriptive; core findings empirical) / 🔴 (design prescriptions normative).
  *What it actually shows:* Survey of workers and developers rating 171 representative tasks,
  then language models used to scale ratings to 10,131 O*NET computer-assisted tasks across
  all US occupations. Primary finding: tasks workers associate with **agency and happiness are
  disproportionately exposed to AI** relative to tasks workers rate as routine or low-meaning.
  Secondary finding: there is a measurable mismatch between what workers want from AI systems
  (straightforward, tolerant, practical) and what developers report emphasising in system design
  (polite, strict, imaginative). *Limits (state honestly):* the "agency/happiness" rating is
  self-reported and the scaling relies on LM inference from those ratings, not independent
  measurement; the CHI venue has a design-advocacy flavour; no causal or longitudinal
  component. *Why it matters:* this is the first systematic empirical mapping of which task
  types are being automated against workers' own assessments of what those tasks mean to them.
  It reframes the meaning-of-work risk from "some jobs will disappear" to "the specific parts
  of jobs that produce wellbeing are disproportionately targeted." Pairs directly with Lee et
  al. (passive AI use erodes self-efficacy/meaning) already in evidence_base — Lee et al.
  shows the *mechanism* at the individual level; this paper shows the *selection* at the
  task/occupational level. The developer–worker preference mismatch is a policy and design
  lever the project can name explicitly.
  → candidate for evidence_base (task-level meaning exposure; pairs with Lee et al.).
  → idea: "what gets automated first is what felt most human" — piece arguing that the
  economic logic of automation targets the highest-value cognitive tasks, which overlap
  heavily with the tasks people draw meaning from; the remedy is not just "retrain" but
  deliberate design choices about which task types are handed to AI.

- **Dallas Fed Texas Business Outlook Survey, May 2026 supplement** — Dallas Fed (late May
  2026; news coverage June 2, 2026). https://www.dallasfed.org/research/surveys/tbos/2026/2605q
  🟢 (employer survey, N > 300 executives; descriptive, not causal; single-state, self-report).
  *What it actually shows:* 66% of Texas firms are using AI to some degree. Among AI-using
  firms: 10% say AI has *already* decreased their need for workers; 76% say no staffing impact
  yet; 30% expect AI to decrease their need for workers in the medium term. Specific tasks
  named by executives as already replaced: accounts payable, order processing, communications,
  marketing, coding, graphic design, customer support, programming. *Limits (state honestly):*
  single-state business survey; self-reported intent; "decreased need for workers" is
  subjective and may not track actual headcount; forward-looking 30% is intention, not outcome.
  *Why it matters:* the task list maps directly onto what the labour literature identifies as
  high AI-exposure work. The gap between current impact (10%) and medium-term expectation
  (30%) reflects the same "early innings / slow roll" story from prior logs, but from the
  employer side and with named tasks. Not a meaning finding per se — more a labour-market
  data point — but grounds the abstract exposure scores in concrete employer behaviour.

## Watchlist movement

- **Automation vs. augmentation (analogy breakpoint #1):** Ranjit et al. adds a new
  sub-dimension: the tasks *being automated* are not random — they are disproportionately the
  ones workers value for agency and meaning. If accurate, this tightens the analogy breakpoint:
  even where augmentation nominally "leads," the specific micro-tasks being offloaded to AI
  are the psychologically salient ones. Watch for independent replication.
- **Speed vs. adjustment (#2):** The Dallas Fed TBOS employer survey shows the current
  impact is still modest (10% reporting reduced headcount need), consistent with the
  prior-log read that macro disruption is not yet in the data. But the 30% medium-term
  expectation suggests the adjustment lag may compress. No new movement beyond what was
  already logged from Anthropic / NY Fed / PIIE.
- **The "new jobs" mechanism (#3):** no new data today; IMF SDN/2026/001 (logged 2026-06-01)
  remains the current best evidence — AI-skill jobs carry wage premium but no net employment
  gain.
