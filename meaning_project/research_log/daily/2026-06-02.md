## Items

- **"From Future of Work to Future of Workers: Addressing Asymptomatic AI Harms for
  Dignified Human-AI Interaction"** — Passi et al., Microsoft Research; arXiv:2601.21920
  (Jan 2026); also in *Proceedings of CHI 2026*.
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.21920
  🟡 (qualitative longitudinal study; interpretive framing; no pre-registration). Year-long
  embedded study of AI use by cancer specialists. *What it actually shows:* AI produced
  visible short-term operational gains while simultaneously causing "intuition rust" — a
  gradual dulling of expert clinical judgment that was invisible in productivity metrics until
  it wasn't. Authors name this pattern "asymptomatic harm": the benefit is measured
  immediately, the cost accrues slowly and is hard to attribute. Secondary finding: repeated
  AI delegation progressively eroded participants' professional identity ("identity
  commoditization"). *Limits (state honestly):* qualitative, single-organisation, single
  domain (oncology) — does not cleanly generalise; the CHI venue favours design-driven
  framings. *Why it matters:* directly extends the Lee et al. passive-use finding (already
  in evidence_base) into a longitudinal, high-stakes professional context, and adds a new
  vocabulary: "asymptomatic harm" and "intuition rust" are useful framings for the project's
  claim that augmentation-vs-automation is a psychological category. The delay between
  benefit and harm is itself relevant to how individuals and policy-makers can recognise the
  problem. Pairs with the Lee et al. 🟢 anchor.
  → idea: "the invisible tax" — piece on how AI's costs to identity and competence trail its
  benefits by months or years, making them systematically under-reported and under-regulated.

- **"Social Connections and Loneliness in OECD Countries"** — OECD Publishing (Oct 2025).
  https://www.oecd.org/en/publications/social-connections-and-loneliness-in-oecd-countries_6df2d6a0-en.html
  🟢 (official cross-national survey data; descriptive/observational — not an experiment).
  Large-sample synthesis of OECD survey data on social connection trends. *What it actually
  shows:* in-person social interactions are declining across OECD countries; self-reported
  loneliness only sharply worsened during COVID; men and young people (16–24) have
  experienced the largest recent deteriorations — young people saw the biggest increase in
  loneliness (+1pp, 2018–2022) of any age group; socioeconomic disadvantage strongly
  predicts worse social-connection outcomes; and loneliness + low social interaction are
  independently associated with ~871,000 premature deaths/year globally. *Limits (state
  honestly):* descriptive trends, not causal; cross-national comparability caveats; the
  871,000 mortality figure is a global estimate under varying methodological assumptions.
  *Why it matters:* adds the demographic precision missing from Jahoda / Case & Deaton: it
  is young men specifically who are socially most at risk — the same group the Stanford/ADP
  data show bearing the early AI employment hit. The intersection (young + male + AI-exposed
  + socially disconnecting) is a concrete population the writing can name, rather than a
  diffuse worry. Also grounds the OECD's formal recognition that loneliness is a policy-
  level priority alongside economic outcomes. The 871k mortality figure pairs with the
  Emile (2026) purpose-erosion mortality data already in evidence_base.
  → candidate for evidence_base (loneliness trends + demographic sharpening).
  → idea: "the double exposure" — young men taking the first employment hit AND the steepest
  loneliness rise; the two pressures compound in exactly the population where meaning-
  through-work was already the main social organiser.

## Watchlist movement

- **Augmentation vs automation (analogy breakpoint #1):** resolved since the 2026-06-01 flag.
  The Anthropic "Learning curves" report (primary, verified 2026-06, now in evidence_base)
  confirms augmentation still leads on the consumer surface; API automation does not
  contaminate the consumer reading. No new movement today.
- **Speed vs adjustment (#2):** the "asymptomatic harm" finding adds a new dimension — the
  adjustment lag is not just economic (retraining takes years) but psychological (harm is
  invisible until it is chronic). The adjustment-speed breakpoint may be worse than the
  standard economic framing suggests.
- **Young workers / entry-level (#3):** OECD loneliness data reinforces the Stanford/ADP
  young-cohort employment finding — the same age group (16–25) is both losing entry-level
  employment footing and experiencing the steepest social disconnection trend.
