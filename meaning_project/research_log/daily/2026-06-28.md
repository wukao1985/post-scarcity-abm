## Watchlist movement

- **New to log (outside window — March 2026):** Yotzov, Barrero, Bloom, Bunn, Davis et al.,
  *"Firm Data on AI"* (NBER WP 34836; Atlanta Fed, Bank of England, Bundesbank, Macquarie;
  first circulated Mar 2026; NBER Digest May 2026;
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w34836).
  🟢 with caveats (first representative multi-country firm survey; ~6,000 CEOs/CFOs across US,
  UK, Germany, Australia, Nov 2025–Jan 2026; stratified sample; but **self-reported expectations**,
  not realised outcomes; one survey round; English-speaking / advanced-economy bias).
  *What it actually shows:* 69% of firms actively use AI; most executives use AI but only ~1.5
  hrs/week; **nine-in-ten executives report no employment or productivity impact over the last
  three years** — consistent with the macro-null cluster already logged (Humlum & Vestergaard,
  Audoly et al., IMF, ILO). But looking ahead, **executives forecast −0.7% own-firm employment
  over the next three years** while **workers at those same firms forecast +0.5%** — a
  conspicuous **employer/worker expectations gap** of ~1.2 percentage points. Neither forecast
  is a realised outcome. Younger, more productive firms adopt faster.
  *Limits to state honestly:* stated expectations have a notoriously poor track record as
  predictors of actual outcomes; the "next three years" window is inherently speculative; the
  employer/worker gap may reflect information asymmetry, optimism bias, or genuine disagreement —
  the data cannot distinguish. Not a 🟢 anchor for job-loss claims; a 🟢 anchor only for the
  *pattern of expectations* at this point in time.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* The employer/worker expectations divergence is new
  information: **workers appear not yet to have internalised the employment risk their employers
  anticipate**. If the employer forecast is closer to correct, the psychological disruption is
  not evenly distributed in anticipation — workers arrive at displacement unprepared. Pairs with
  the "rapid currents beneath still waters" framing (Humlum & Vestergaard) and reinforces the
  ILO J-curve's implication: the macro is still calm, but internal firm logic is already pricing
  in contraction. Not an evidence-base candidate yet (expectations only; one round); flag for
  longitudinal tracking.

- **New to log (outside window — March 2026, revised June 3):** Falk & Tsoukalas (UPenn /
  Boston University), *"The AI Layoff Trap"* (arXiv:2603.20617; BusinessWire press release
  Jun 10, 2026; McEntire sensitivity analysis SSRN:6592220, Apr 2026;
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.20617).
  🟡 (economic theory paper — game-theoretic model, not empirical; the core mechanism is a
  formal argument, not a measurement; **McEntire replication shows results are
  parameter-sensitive**, so treat as a conceptual frame, not a quantitative prediction).
  *What it actually argues:* In a competitive task-based model, each firm captures the **full
  cost saving** from automating a task but bears only a **fraction of the demand loss** it
  creates — the rest lands on rival firms whose workers spend less. This "demand externality"
  traps rational firms in an automation arms race that produces displacement **beyond the social
  optimum** — a deadweight loss for both workers *and* firm owners. Tested six widely-cited
  policy fixes: UBI, capital income taxes, worker equity, upskilling/retraining, Coasean
  bargaining, and a Pigouvian automation tax. Only the **automation tax** corrects the
  distortion; the other five leave each firm's automation incentive unchanged. *McEntire's
  sensitivity analysis confirms the internal logic but shows the sign and magnitude depend
  entirely on parameter choices — under standard alternative parameters the same model produces
  stability or under-automation.* So the trap is possible, not inevitable.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* The Ch.7 "robust bet" layer discusses policy
  responses to displacement. This paper adds a **policy-economics argument** that UBI alone —
  absent a tax on automation — does not change the automation incentive structure. That is a
  useful conceptual point even if the quantitative result is parameter-sensitive: the
  *distributional* fix (UBI) and the *incentive* fix (automation tax or equivalent) are
  logically separable. If the trap mechanism is real, then the policy package that handles both
  displacement and meaning is necessarily multi-instrument. The McEntire caveat is essential to
  state. Use as 🟡 conceptual scaffolding for Ch.7, not as a 🟢 empirical finding.
  → idea: "the two-instrument problem" — even if UBI handles the income/material-needs gap,
  it cannot by itself correct the automation over-incentive; addressing both displacement and
  meaning may require instruments targeting different parts of the problem simultaneously.
