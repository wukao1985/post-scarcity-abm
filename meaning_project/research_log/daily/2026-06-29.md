## Watchlist movement

- **New to log (outside window — Feb 2025; resolves the Jun 26 primary-source flag):** Jiang,
  Park, Xiao & Zhang, *"AI and the Extended Workday: Productivity, Contracting Efficiency,
  and Distribution of Rents"* (Columbia / USC; NBER WP 33536, Feb 2025; SSRN:5119118; data
  from American Time Use Survey 2004–2023; https://www.nber.org/papers/w33536).
  🟢 with caveats (individual-level time-use diary data; ChatGPT shock used as natural
  experiment; **data ends 2023** — narrow post-adoption window; "satisfaction" not directly
  measured in ATUS — see limits; NBER working paper, not yet peer-reviewed).
  *What it actually shows:* Higher AI occupational exposure is associated with **longer work
  hours and reduced leisure** after the ChatGPT shock. The mechanism is **complement-driven**:
  AI raises productivity per unit of effort; under prevailing contracting arrangements this
  induces more hours demanded, not fewer. The "distribution of rents" finding: productivity
  gains from AI accrue primarily to **firms**, not to workers in the form of reduced hours
  or higher leisure. *Limits to state honestly:* ATUS does not measure job satisfaction or
  subjective wellbeing directly — the "satisfaction decrease" claim in the Brookings June 25
  synthesis (logged June 26) may be from a separate underlying study; do not conflate. Data
  ends 2023, so the post-ChatGPT window is narrow. Effects estimated at the occupation level,
  not individual AI adoption. "ChatGPT shock" identification assumes exogeneity of time-use
  trends — plausible but not independently verified from this summary. NBER working paper
  only.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* This is the primary empirical basis for the
  "work-intensification mechanism" flagged on June 26 as needing retrieval. It opens a
  **third "work reshaped in meaning-diminishing directions" pathway** alongside Lee et al.
  (passive AI use → self-efficacy/meaning erosion) and ILO WP170 (algorithmic management
  → autonomy erosion): AI can erode meaning-sustaining non-work time even for workers who
  keep their jobs and even when AI appears to be augmenting rather than automating them.
  The rent-distribution finding reinforces the Falk & Tsoukalas "two-instrument problem"
  (logged June 28): a distributional fix (UBI or income support) does not correct the
  incentive for firms to extract more hours per productive worker. The meaning implication:
  post-work *time* is also being eroded — the space where purpose, relationships, and
  recovery might otherwise occur contracts alongside task autonomy and competence. Caveat:
  the Brookings satisfaction claim still needs a separate primary source.
  → candidate for evidence_base (work-intensification mechanism; complement-based extension
  of work hours; rent capture by firms; pairs with Lee et al. and ILO WP170 for the "work
  reshaped in meaning-diminishing directions" cluster).

- **New to log (outside window — January 2026):** Manning & Aguirre, *"How Adaptable Are
  American Workers to AI-Induced Job Displacement?"* (Sam J. Manning, Tomás Aguirre; NBER
  WP 34705, Jan 2026; https://www.nber.org/papers/w34705).
  🟡/🟢 (novel adaptive-capacity index covering 356 occupations, 95.9% of US workforce;
  **prospective framing** — displacement at scale has not occurred, so this measures
  pre-displacement characteristics, not post-displacement outcomes; "adaptive capacity" is
  a constructed index, not observed wellbeing).
  *What it actually shows:* Builds an occupational adaptive capacity index (skills
  transferability, geographic mobility, retraining access, labour-market slack in destination
  occupations) and cross-maps it against AI exposure. Key finding: AI exposure and adaptive
  capacity are *positively correlated* — 26.5 million of 37.1 million top-quartile-exposed
  workers are in occupations with above-median adaptive capacity. But **6.1 million workers
  (4.2% of the workforce)** are in a "double-jeopardy" zone: both highly exposed *and*
  below-median adaptive capacity. This group is concentrated in **clerical and administrative
  roles** (data entry, office support, bookkeeping). *Limits:* Adaptive capacity is modelled
  prospectively, not measured from actual displacement outcomes. The index captures economic
  transition ability, not psychological resilience or meaning-maintenance capacity. Being
  in an above-median-adaptive occupation does not guarantee any individual displaced worker
  will adapt; the 6.1M is an aggregate estimate under current conditions. Not peer-reviewed.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* The clerical/admin "double-jeopardy" cohort is
  **distinct from the "young college graduates"** thread (Stanford/ADP, NY Fed). This is an
  older, more economically marginal group — historically one for whom work has supplied
  Jahoda's latent functions (structure, status, social contact, purpose) without the
  professional-identity buffer of knowledge workers. If the 6.1M figure is roughly correct,
  it marks a concentrated vulnerability population whose displacement would combine economic
  severity with the weakest capacity to substitute purpose or structure through re-employment.
  The meaning cost is likely concentrated precisely here — not among the adaptable
  knowledge-worker majority. Useful for the policy chapter's "who bears the meaning cost"
  framing. Not yet an evidence-base candidate (prospective model, not outcome data).
