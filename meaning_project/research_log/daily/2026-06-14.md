## Items

- **"Crashing Waves vs. Rising Tides: Preliminary Findings on AI Automation from Thousands
  of Worker Evaluations of Labor Market Tasks"** — Matthias Mertens, Adam Kuzee, et al.;
  MIT FutureTech; arXiv:2604.01363, April 2026.
  https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.01363
  🟢 (with caveats: empirical worker evaluations of specific tasks — 17,000+ individual
  ratings across 3,000+ text-based O*NET tasks — not a theoretical index; but the 2029
  projections are extrapolations, the paper is a preprint titled "preliminary findings,"
  and the success-rate benchmarks depend on how "task completion" is defined and by whom;
  not yet peer-reviewed or independently replicated).
  *What it actually shows:* The paper evaluates AI capabilities against 3,000+ text-based
  tasks drawn from the US Department of Labor's O*NET database, using 17,000+ evaluations by
  workers drawn from the occupations where those tasks appear (i.e. workers in the relevant
  jobs rating whether AI can currently complete what they actually do). It tests two models of
  how AI capability expands: (a) **"crashing waves"** — sudden, concentrated surges over
  narrow task sets (the intuition behind "AI will eliminate entire occupations at once"), and
  (b) **"rising tides"** — gradual, broad-based capability improvement across nearly all
  text-based task types simultaneously. Core finding: little evidence of crashing waves;
  strong evidence that rising tides dominate. Quantitative benchmarks: as of Q2 2024, AI
  models successfully completed tasks that take humans approximately 3–4 hours at ~50% of
  the time; by Q3 2025, that figure rose to ~65%. The paper projects 80–95% success rates
  by 2029, though this is an extrapolation. The "rising tides" pattern means AI is not
  surgically removing one occupation at a time but gradually raising its competency floor
  across the full spectrum of text-based work simultaneously. *Limits (state honestly):* The
  paper is explicitly titled "preliminary findings" and is a preprint — the methodology is
  novel and has not been independently replicated; "task completion" success rates depend on
  how tasks are defined and how workers judge completion, introducing substantial subjectivity;
  the task sample is text-based O*NET tasks only — physical, in-person, and relational tasks
  are excluded by construction; the 2029 projection is a simple extrapolation of observed
  Q2 2024 → Q3 2025 trajectory, with no uncertainty interval visible in secondary coverage;
  the acknowledgment of David Autor suggests friendly review, not independent replication;
  worker evaluations of whether AI "can" do their tasks may be contaminated by unfamiliarity
  with current AI capabilities in either direction. *Why it matters for the meaning project:*
  The crashing-waves-vs-rising-tides distinction directly addresses the MANIFESTO's
  **"speed vs adjustment"** analogy breakpoint — the critical question of whether AI capability
  expands fast enough relative to economic and psychological adjustment timescales to produce
  widespread meaning disruption before new structures emerge. The "rising tides" finding cuts
  two ways: it weakens the "sudden shock" / displacement-wave narrative (adjustment may be
  more gradual than feared), but it also means there is no safe occupational refuge in
  cognitive/text-based work — the rising tide reaches everywhere in that domain, including
  the "meaning-heavy" tasks (creative, analytical, advisory) that workers tend to retain last.
  This connects directly to Ranjit et al.'s finding (in evidence_base) that AI disproportionately
  targets the tasks workers rate as high-agency and high-happiness: if rising tides reach those
  tasks gradually but inevitably, the "automating the joy out of work" trajectory runs on a
  slower but broader front than crashing-wave models imply. For the project's watchlist: the
  50% → 65% success rate over ~5 quarters is a concrete pace signal; if that trajectory holds,
  the 2029 projection is the next meaningful horizon check.
  → candidate for evidence_base (methodology: worker evaluations of task completion vs
  theoretical exposure scoring; rising-tides pattern; quantified capability trajectory;
  pairs with Ranjit et al. on the intersection of capability expansion and meaning-laden tasks).
  → idea: "the tide that reaches everywhere" — if AI capability expands as rising tides
  (broad, gradual, across all text-based task types) rather than crashing waves (sudden
  occupational wipeouts), the adjustment problem is different: not "which occupation survives?"
  but "what does human contribution consist of when AI's text-based competency floor is
  at 80–95%?" The meaning question becomes more pressing, not less, under rising tides.

## Watchlist movement

- **Speed vs. adjustment (MANIFESTO analogy breakpoint):** The MIT "rising tides" paper is

  Q2 2024→Q3 2025 is a new concrete data point for this breakpoint. Rising tides also changes
  the adjustment challenge: it is not "retrain for a different occupation" but "clarify what
