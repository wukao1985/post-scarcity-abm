## Watchlist movement

- **New to log (outside window, April 28):** Chueri, Gandenberger, Taylor, Knotz & Fossati,
  "AI, the Future of Work, and the Politics of the Welfare State" (*Perspectives on Politics*,
  Cambridge, April 28, 2026; replication data on Harvard Dataverse; 2024 OECD "Risks that
  Matter" survey; https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/ai-the-future-of-work-and-the-politics-of-the-welfare-state/92E806B812B86A8944003B77038DCAA8).
  🟡/🟢 (academic political science, published peer-reviewed journal; relies on cross-national
  survey attitudinal data — observational, not causal; OECD survey well-regarded; full primary
  text not retrieved directly, specifics from Cambridge abstract and secondary coverage).
  *What it actually shows:* Fear of AI automation is widespread across educational groups in
  OECD countries; critically, these fears do **not** primarily increase support for traditional
  income-support interventions (unemployment benefits, retraining programs). Instead, they
  drive demand for **measures that preserve the social role of work** — robot taxes, guaranteed
  income regardless of employment status. *Limits:* attitudinal data, not behavioural; the
  survey captures stated preferences under uncertainty, not revealed preferences; cross-national
  comparability caveats apply; "Risks that Matter" 2024 data predates the post-ChatGPT period's
  full policy debate; whether preferences translate to political pressure is an open question.
  *Relevance for the meaning project:* This is an empirical counterpart to the MANIFESTO's
  core question — and the public's answer is not "just replace the income." Survey respondents
  implicitly recognise work's latent social/meaning functions (Jahoda) and want to preserve
  work's *role*, not only its income stream. Directly relevant to Ch.7's "robust bet" and the
  project's 🔴 policy layer: public appetite for robot taxes and work-preserving measures is
  more widespread than policy discourse typically assumes, and cuts across class/education
  lines. Also provides a political-economy counterweight to the techno-optimist reading that
  income support alone will do. **Flag for primary-text retrieval:** if the Cambridge full
  text is accessible, this deserves a proper evidence-base entry — it provides rare 🟢-quality
  *political science* data (not only economics or psychology) on how populations relate to the
  social role of work in the AI era.
