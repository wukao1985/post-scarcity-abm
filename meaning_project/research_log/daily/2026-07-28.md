## Items

- **"Rethinking Automation Risk: AI Applicability and Occupational Outcomes, 2019–24"** — Kristen
  Broady, Caleb Dunson & Anthony Barr, Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago Working Paper 2026-12
  (published 2 Jul 2026; verified from primary PDF, doi.org/10.21033/wp-2026-12). 🟢 **descriptive,
  not causal** (real BLS occupational employment/wage data + O*NET, 2019–2024; authors explicitly
  state "these findings do not prove that automation or AI caused the observed employment or wage
  changes" and flag DiD designs as future work for causal attribution; the window includes the
  COVID shock as a major confound; N is small at the occupation level — e.g. the "high AI
  applicability" group is only 38 occupations). *Shows:* using Tomlinson et al.'s (2025)
  Copilot-usage-based "AI applicability" scores (a deployment/augmentation-potential measure)
  matched to occupations, employment in the highest-applicability occupations *grew* 4.05%
  (335,800 jobs) and wages grew 21.7%, 2019–2024 — while occupations with the *lowest*
  applicability scores took a sharper COVID-era hit and still sat slightly below their 2019
  employment level in 2024. Separately, using Frey & Osborne's (2017) classic *computerization-risk*
  scores, employment fell on average in high-risk (−3%) and moderate-risk (−6%) occupations while
  growing in low-risk ones (+8%) — but wages rose in *all three* risk tiers (21–26%), and within
  every risk tier the variance swamps the average (e.g. "high risk" spans telemarketers at −51%
  employment to models at +131%). *Why it matters:* this is a genuinely new **primary-source**
  reconciliation of a tension already live in the evidence base — it shows that *which* exposure
  metric you pick (usage-based "applicability" vs. task-based "computerizability") can flip the
  sign of the employment story entirely, on the same underlying occupations, over the same years.
  That sharpens rather than resolves the augmentation-vs-automation watchlist question (does the
  "AI helps most where it's used most" story hold, or does classic automation risk still bite?) —
  and is a caution against citing any single exposure-score headline (including this project's own
  citations of Stanford/ADP, ECB, or Census BTOS) without naming which construct is doing the work.
  Note for accuracy: the CFO Dive coverage that surfaced this ("Jobs in occupations most exposed to
  AI increased by 4%") reported the applicability-score employment figure correctly but did not
  mention the opposite-signed computerization-risk result in the same paper — a reminder to read
  primary text rather than headlines, exactly as this project's own honesty rule requires.
  → not yet a candidate for evidence_base (descriptive/non-causal, single working paper, COVID
  confound); worth a second look if a causal (DiD) follow-up appears, per the authors' own stated
  next step.
