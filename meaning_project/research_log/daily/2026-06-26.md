## Watchlist movement

- **New to log (June 25, within window — secondary synthesis):** Brookings Institution,
  "The effects of AI on firms and workers" (brookings.edu, 25 Jun 2026;
  https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-effects-of-ai-on-firms-and-workers/).
  🟡 (think-tank synthesis; no primary data; primary studies underlying each claim not
  identified from available summaries — treat as indicative, not 🟢).
  *What it actually shows:* A Brookings synthesis of recent firm- and worker-level AI studies.
  Key reported findings: (a) most firm-level studies find productivity benefits, with exceptions
  (AI slowed developers in one case; consultants over-relied on AI outside its capability
  range); (b) employment fell more for young workers in AI-exposed occupations; (c) freelancers
  in AI-exposed occupations experienced approximately 2% fewer contracts and 5% lower earnings
  post-2022; (d) **"AI exposure increases work hours and decreases employee satisfaction."**
  *Limits:* Secondary synthesis from a centre with AI-research funding ties; underlying primary
  studies for finding (d) not identified; "employee satisfaction" measure and study design
  unverifiable from summaries.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* Finding (d) — AI *increases* work hours while
  *decreasing* satisfaction — is new to the log and directionally important. If replicated from
  primary sources, it adds a **work-intensification mechanism** to the meaning picture: AI
  doesn't simply substitute work but may add monitoring/review burden that consumes time while
  eroding psychological reward. This pairs with ILO WP170 (algorithmic management → autonomy
  erosion) and Ranjit et al. (agency-laden tasks are the ones AI targets). The finding is
  potentially a bridge between the "task-substitution" frame and the "work-reshaped-in-meaning-
  diminishing-directions" frame. **Flag for primary-source retrieval**: identify which specific
  study Brookings is citing for the work-hours / satisfaction claim before any 🟢 use.
  Findings (b) and (c) are consistent with the Stanford/ADP Canaries paper (already logged,
  with the remote-work confound caveat) and the Siddiq & Zhang Upwork commoditization paper
  (logged June 25) respectively — no new information there.
