## Items

- **"Loneliness — not social isolation — predicts cognitive impairment and shorter life"** —
  Tomiko Yoneda et al.; *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* (published 15 Jun 2026).
  UC Davis press release: https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/loneliness-drives-cognitive-impairment-and-shorter-life-more-social-isolation-new-study
  🟢 (large cross-national sample, prestigious journal, NIH-funded; *but* observational, older-
  adult sample mean 50+, loneliness measured as self-reported frequency — primary text not
  retrieved directly; specifics corroborated from UC Davis press release only).
  *What it actually shows:* In 175,000 adults 50+ across 18 countries, subjective loneliness
  (the *feeling* of disconnection) was consistently associated with higher risk of severe
  cognitive impairment — a 10% rise in reported loneliness correlated with an 8–9% higher
  risk — and with shorter life; it also reduced the chance of recovering from mild impairment
  to normal cognition by ~3%. Social isolation (objective lack of social contact) showed weak
  associations with shorter life and *no* consistent link to cognitive decline. The key
  distinction, in the authors' words: "Loneliness is a perception…You could be surrounded by a
  crowd of people and still feel lonely, whereas isolation is just being alone."
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* This adds a third health stake to Q3's
  relatedness/loneliness thread — cognitive decline, alongside purpose-erosion (Emile 2026)
  and premature mortality (OECD, Folk & Dunn). More importantly, the *subjective/objective
  distinction* sharpens the mechanism: it is the *felt* experience of disconnection that drives
  harm, not physical aloneness per se. This bears directly on the AI-companions argument:
  companionship apps and chatbots may reduce objective isolation (you are "talking to someone")
  while leaving the subjective feeling of loneliness unchanged or worsened — which is precisely
  what Folk & Dunn (prospective panel, *Psychological Science* 2026) and the Aalto Replika
  study (CHI 2026) find empirically. The Yoneda study now tells us the pathogenic variable is
  the feeling, not the contact; the companion studies tell us AI does not reliably address the
  feeling and may worsen it. Together they close the loop: AI companions target the wrong
  variable, and the right variable is the one with the serious health consequences.
  The cognitive-decline dimension also adds to the stakes of the work-and-meaning erosion
  argument — alongside Kouchekinia et al.'s finding that involuntary job loss causally
  accelerates cognitive decline (~2 extra years of ageing), we now have a pathway from
  loneliness to the same outcome; given that work is one of the primary sources of *both*
  structured social contact *and* sense of purpose, its erosion threatens the loneliness-
  prevention function as well as the purpose function.
  → candidate for evidence_base (add to Q3/relatedness section: subjective loneliness, not
  objective isolation, is the variable that predicts cognitive impairment and shorter life;
  175,000 adults, 18 countries, JPSP 2026; pairs with Emile purpose-mortality mediation and
  the AI-companion crowd-out findings).
  → idea: "wrong variable" — if AI companions reduce isolation (contact) but not loneliness
  (felt connection), and loneliness is the variable that actually matters for health, then the
  companion-as-solution pitch is solving the wrong problem at scale.

- **Relatedness / Q3 (loneliness stakes):** The subjective/objective distinction in Yoneda et
  al. adds nuance to the Q3 picture: the project's claim that AI companions are inadequate as
  a loneliness fix now has a more precise formulation — they may reduce *isolation* (contact)
  but not *loneliness* (felt connection), and it is loneliness that drives cognitive and
  mortality harm. The three-study convergence (Folk & Dunn panel, Aalto quasi-experiment, Li
  et al. RCT) established AI companions fail to fix loneliness; this study establishes why
