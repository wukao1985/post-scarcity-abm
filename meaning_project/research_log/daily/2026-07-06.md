## Items

- **Anthropic Economic Index — "Cadences" report** (Anthropic, published 26 Jun 2026;
  https://www.anthropic.com/research/economic-index-june-2026-report). 🟡 (self-report survey;
  single-provider; explicitly correlational — the authors themselves flag the confound, see
  below). *What it actually shows:* a linked survey (N≈9,700; launched Apr 2026; usage data
  linked mid-May–early-Jun 2026; Claude.ai + Cowork + Claude Code; **not representative** —
  skews computer/mathematical (30%) and management (23%) occupations, filtered to active users)
  found that respondents with a **higher "automation share"** of their Claude conversations
  (tasks delegated with minimal iterative input, vs. augmentation-style iterative collaboration)
  reported **more optimistic expectations across all six measured dimensions** — pay, job
  security, ability to find new jobs (economic), *and* **meaning, autonomy, human interaction**
  (intrinsic) — with the largest effects on pay and employment prospects. The authors state
  plainly: *"It's possible that this relationship is explained by selection, that the people
  most enthusiastic about AI are also the most willing to hand over entire tasks to it"* — i.e.
  they do not claim automation *causes* optimism. Separately: 10% rated their own job loss
  "likely/very likely" within 12 months; over a third worried junior colleagues faced >60%
  job-loss probability; early-career workers reported the highest displacement anxiety;
  experienced workers (15yr+) cited judgment/context/interpersonal skill as AI-resistant.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* this is a **self-report, correlational counterpoint**
  to Lee et al.'s experimental finding already anchored in evidence_base.md (passive/automated
  AI use *causally* reduces self-efficacy, ownership and felt meaning, vs. active collaboration).
  Here, heavier automators report feeling *better* about meaning/autonomy — but this is
  *expected/perceived* meaning from a survey the authors admit is selection-confounded, not a
  measured psychological outcome from an experiment. The two findings are not necessarily in
  tension (Lee et al. measures felt meaning *during/after* a task; Cadences measures *general
  optimism about the future* among people who already chose to delegate heavily) but they sit
  in the same augmentation-vs-automation-as-psychology space and should not be conflated when
  cited. *Limits to state honestly:* unrepresentative sample (skews tech/management, active
  Claude users only); single-provider; authors' own selection-confound caveat is load-bearing,
  not decorative; "automation share" is a behavioural proxy, not a validated construct;
  optimism ≠ meaning itself. *Not a 🟢 evidence-base candidate* (self-report, author-flagged
  confound) — logged as a 🟡 nuance to the existing Lee et al./Ranjit et al./Giuntella et al.
  cluster on how AI use-mode shapes psychological outcomes.
  → candidate: add as a caveat/counterpoint note under the "augmentation/automation axis as
  psychology" section of evidence_base.md, explicitly distinguishing measured-outcome (Lee et
  al.) from self-reported-expectation (this survey).
