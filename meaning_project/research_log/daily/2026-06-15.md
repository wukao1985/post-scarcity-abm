## Items

- **"AI systems @ work: a changing psychosocial work environment"** — International Labour
  Organization; Working Paper 170 (WP170), 30 April 2026.
  https://www.ilo.org/publications/ai-systems-work-changing-psychosocial-work-environment
  🟡 (synthesis working paper from a credible international institution, not primary
  experimental data; honesty note: full primary text not retrieved — content is reconstructed
  from the ILO press release and secondary coverage; treat as indicative until verified from
  primary text).
  *What it actually shows:* The working paper examines how AI deployments — specifically
  algorithmic management systems and AI-driven workplace surveillance — are reshaping the
  psychosocial work environment. Core finding reported by ILO: AI-driven intrusive surveillance
  and loss of worker autonomy are linked to psychosocial risks including monitoring anxiety,
  erosion of dignity and privacy, and reduced sense of control over one's own work. Technostress
  and work intensification are identified as consistent patterns across the evidence surveyed.
  *Limits (state honestly):* Working paper format — not peer-reviewed primary data; the ILO
  mandate (worker protection) shapes what risks get foregrounded; full text not retrieved, so
  what "linked to psychosocial risks" means quantitatively — effect sizes, study quality,
  confidence levels — is not verifiable from search summaries alone; the surveillance findings
  may not generalise evenly across labour markets with different regulatory environments.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* This paper introduces a mechanism not yet in the
  log: AI as a management and control tool deployed *on* workers, not only as a task-substitution
  tool. Autonomy is one of SDT's three basic psychological needs (alongside competence and
  relatedness); evidence that algorithmic management erodes autonomy satisfaction extends the
  Lee et al. finding (passive AI use → reduced meaning at the individual task level) to a
  structural/systemic level — it is not only about how individual workers choose to use AI
  tools, but how employers deploy AI *against* workers' sense of agency. For employed workers
  who are not displaced, algorithmic surveillance may corrode precisely the autonomy that
  sustains intrinsic motivation and meaning. Pairs with and extends Ranjit et al. ("automating
  the joy out of work") and the BCG June 8 log ("AI frees time but organisations don't redesign
  work"): the three together sketch a picture where AI reshapes work in meaning-diminishing
  directions across multiple levels (task selection, time use, and now control architecture).
  The ILO's institutional framing also matters for Ch.7's policy chapter: if the SDT autonomy
  threat arrives via management architecture, the relevant intervention is labour-law and
  algorithmic-management regulation, not just income support or retraining.
  → idea: "two threats, one mechanism" — AI reshapes meaning via displacement (removing
  task competence and structured purpose) AND via surveillance (removing autonomy from workers
  who remain employed); the SDT needs-satisfaction picture worsens on both paths, and policy
  that addresses only income replacement misses the second threat entirely.
