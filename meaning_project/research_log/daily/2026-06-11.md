## Items

- **"Is a random human peer better than a highly supportive chatbot in reducing loneliness
  over time?"** — Li, R.-N., Folk, D., Singh, A., Ungar, L. & Dunn, E.; *Journal of
  Experimental Social Psychology* vol. 125, art. 104911 (2026).
  https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022103126000417
  🟢 (pre-registered three-arm RCT; N=296 first-year students; materials & code on OSF;
  single university, limited to a 2-week window, loneliness as sole primary outcome, sample
  skews young/high-baseline-lonely; not yet independently replicated).
  *What it actually shows:* Participants assigned for two weeks to one of: (a) text daily
  with a randomly matched human peer (a stranger), (b) text daily with "Sam," a chatbot
  purpose-built using relationship-science principles to be maximally empathic and supportive,
  or (c) write a daily journal entry (active control). Result: only the human-peer arm showed
  a statistically significant reduction in loneliness by week two. The chatbot performed
  indistinguishably from the journaling control — it was no more effective than writing to
  no one. *Limits (state honestly):* single university (UBC), first-year student sample
  (high baseline loneliness, narrow age band), 2-week intervention window is too short to
  test sustained effects, self-report loneliness only, "Sam" is a custom chatbot rather than
  a deployed commercial product (limits direct generalisation to e.g. Replika or ChatGPT
  used as companion), expectation effects not fully ruled out, no follow-up. *Why it
  matters:* the evidence_base already carries the Aalto/CHI 2026 quasi-experiment showing
  AI companion use crowds out human connection over time (Reddit language proxy) and the
  2025 human-empathy preference study (labelled-AI responses rated less empathic). This Li
  et al. RCT adds the **causal** layer the prior studies lack: a pre-registered experiment
  with an active head-to-head comparison under controlled conditions. The key result — that
  a *random stranger* (not a chosen friend, not a counsellor) reliably reduced loneliness
  while a purpose-built empathic chatbot did not move the needle at all — sets a demanding
  empirical benchmark. For the project's Q3 ("can AI supply mattering?"), this tightens the
  answer from "evidence leans no" toward "experimental evidence says no, even under
  optimistic chatbot-design conditions."
  → candidate for evidence_base (adds pre-registered RCT arm to Q3 / relatedness section;
  pairs with Aalto quasi-experiment and 2025 empathy-preference study; now three independent
  designs pointing the same direction).
  → idea: "the stranger works" — the striking implication is that the bar for human
  connection is surprisingly low (a random stranger you text for two weeks reduces
  loneliness), while a sophisticated AI purpose-built for support fails to clear it at all;
  this inverts the standard "AI as scalable loneliness fix" pitch and suggests cheap
  human-matching interventions may outperform costly AI deployments.

- **"How Does Turning to AI for Companionship Predict Loneliness and Vice Versa?"** — Folk,
  D. & Dunn, E.; *Psychological Science* (2026). https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261427747
  🟢 (12-month longitudinal panel; N>2,000; four waves ~3 months apart; four English-
  speaking countries; prospective design with direct loneliness measurement; observational
  — confounding possible; self-report only; no objective behavioural data on chatbot use).
  *What it actually shows:* Followed 2,000+ adults for one year, measuring social chatbot
  use and loneliness every ~3 months. Both directions of the loop were significant: loneliness
  at time T predicted turning to AI companionship at T+1 (the intuitive direction — lonely
  people seek chatbots), but AI companionship use at T also predicted *higher* loneliness at
  T+1. The worsening loop runs forward in time. Proposed mechanism: AI companions are always
  available and simulate connection compellingly in the moment, meeting the immediate craving
  for contact without building the skill or habit of human relationship maintenance, which
  over time deepens the deficit. *Limits (state honestly):* observational — reverse causality
  and unmeasured confounders cannot be fully ruled out despite the prospective design
  (selection into chatbot use by deepening distress remains plausible); self-report loneliness
  and self-report chatbot use; does not distinguish types of chatbot use (emotional support vs.
  task assistance vs. entertainment); four English-speaking countries limits generalisability;
  the bidirectional mechanism is the authors' hypothesis and would need experimental
  confirmation. *Why it matters:* the Aalto paper in evidence_base demonstrated the
  worsening-over-time pattern using Reddit language as a proxy in Replika users; this Folk &
  Dunn study uses direct loneliness measurement across a much larger, more geographically
  diverse sample in a flagship psychology journal. Two different methods, same direction.
  The specific addition here is the **bidirectional loop**: by showing that AI use predicts
  *subsequent* loneliness (not only concurrent), the study provides a prospective mechanism
  for why deployed AI-companion interventions may systematically worsen population-level
  loneliness — the very people who adopt them (lonely individuals) are the ones for whom
  the worsening loop runs.
  → candidate for evidence_base update: add Folk & Dunn as second converging study alongside
  Aalto in the Q3 / relatedness section; note that with Aalto (quasi-experiment, Reddit
  proxy), Folk & Dunn (prospective panel, direct measures), and Li et al. (RCT), three
  independent designs now converge; together they justify upgrading the Q3 summary from
  "evidence leans no" to "consistent multi-method evidence: AI cannot supply relatedness
  and deployed as a loneliness fix it likely worsens the deficit."
