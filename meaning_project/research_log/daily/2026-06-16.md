## Items

- **"AI reshapes global labour market into two distinct paths, rewarding human skills"** —
  PwC 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer; released 15 June 2026; analysis of >1 billion job
  advertisements across 27 countries and territories.
  https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html
  🟡 (descriptive job-ad analysis from a major consulting firm with a prominent conflict of
  interest — PwC sells AI advisory and implementation services; the report is not peer-reviewed
  and the methodology is proprietary; job ads ≠ filled roles or actual employment outcomes;
  treat findings as directionally suggestive, not causally established).
  *What it actually shows:* Jobs requiring AI skills grew 69% vs 9% for the total market
  (roughly 8× faster); the wage premium for AI skills rose to 62% (up from 56% in 2025).
  The most striking entry-level finding: AI-exposed entry-level roles grew 35% since 2019,
  while non-AI-exposed entry-level roles declined 10% — but the growth comes at a cost: those
  AI-exposed entry-level roles are now seven times more likely to require traditionally
  senior-level skills (judgment, leadership, creativity). The barometer coins a
  "professionalised" vs "democratised" two-track distinction: professionalised roles
  (radiologists, senior recruiters) saw twice the job growth and 42% faster salary growth
  than democratised roles (IT service managers, medical secretaries). Companies most exposed to
  AI recorded 40% higher productivity growth than the least exposed.
  *Limits (state honestly):* The conflict-of-interest is structural — PwC profits from AI
  adoption and has incentive to tell a skill-complementarity story; the "billion job ads"
  methodology is undisclosed and proprietary; job postings data skews toward formal, advertised
  work and misses gig/informal labour; the 35% entry-level growth figure sits in meaningful
  tension with the NY Fed job-postings paper (Audoly et al., evidence base) which found no
  junior/senior vacancy divergence in high-exposure occupations, and with the IMF
  SDN/2026/001 finding that AI-skill postings carry wage premiums but *not* employment gains;
  PwC's headline ("rewarding human skills") papers over the skills-bar-raising mechanism that
  its own data reveals; "professionalised/democratised" is PwC's construct, not an independent
  validated taxonomy; no causal identification.
  *Why it matters for the meaning project:* Despite the source caveats, the Barometer is the
  most geographically extensive job-ads dataset in the log (27 countries, 1B+ ads) and the
  "skills-bar raised at entry level" finding is empirically distinct from the prior evidence base.
  The entry-level picture it describes — existing roles survive but now demand seven times the
  senior-skill competency — is a direct extension of the experience-creep/entry-door thread
  (Stanford/ADP, Massenkoff & McCrory) from a different methodology. Crucially, this has a
  psychological-meaning dimension the economics literature ignores: if a formally "entry-level"
  role now demands judgment and leadership that take years to develop, the standard path by
  which young workers build competence (SDT's competence need) through structured early-career
  work is disrupted — not because the job is eliminated, but because the task complexity
  outpaces the developmental stage. The professionalised/democratised split also gives
  vocabulary for the project's augmentation/automation axis: professionalised roles appear to
  be the augmentation path; democratised roles are where automation substitution concentrates.
  Whether this two-track divergence is an AI effect or a broader credentialisation trend cannot
  be established from this data. The tension with NY Fed and IMF is genuine and unresolved.
  → idea: "the raised floor" — the entry-level meaning problem may not be "the door is
  closed" (NY Fed finds no junior/senior divergence in vacancies) but "the floor of expected
  competence has been raised to a height that takes years to reach" — disrupting the
  developmental arc of early-career meaning-building that work typically provides.
