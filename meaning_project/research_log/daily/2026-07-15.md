## Items

- **"Are the Careers of Older Workers Being Cut Short by AI?"** — Geoffrey T. Sanzenbacher, Center
  for Retirement Research at Boston College, *Issue in Brief* 26-13 (June 2026; surfaced via CNBC
  coverage 13 Jul 2026, read directly from the CRR primary PDF). 🟢 **with caveats** (CPS
  longitudinal panel, 2014–2026, N=102,097 observations of workers 55+, matched to occupation-level
  AI exposure from Tufts' Digital Planet Initiative; probit regression with a pre/post-ChatGPT
  interaction term, extensive demographic/job controls; author's own caveats below). *Shows:*
  **before** ChatGPT, older workers in more AI-exposed occupations were *significantly less likely*
  to leave work than peers in low-exposure jobs (the normal "high-skill jobs have longer careers"
  pattern) — a 1-SD exposure increase associated with **−0.55pp** probability of exiting work.
  **After** ChatGPT's Nov-2022 launch, that protective gap nearly vanished: a 1-SD exposure increase
  is now associated with **+0.50pp** higher exit probability, driven specifically by a
  **+0.28pp rise in unemployment** (involuntary job loss) — **not** by a rise in retirement or
  voluntary labour-force exit (those bars are near-zero/non-significant). For computer programmers
  specifically, predicted transitions to non-employment rose from 8.7% (pre) to 11.1% (post) — a
  >25% relative increase; for painters (low exposure), 13.5%→13.7% (~2%). *Limits (author's own):*
  correlational, not a clean natural experiment — the author explicitly flags that Trump-administration
  R&D cuts hitting AI-exposed sectors could exaggerate the estimate, while the AI-startup hiring boom
  (e.g. data scientists) could understate it; occupation-to-Census-code crosswalk is imperfect; single
  country (US); no data past April 2025 second-observation window. *Why it matters for the project:*
  this is the **older-worker mirror** of the already-anchored Stanford/ADP young-cohort finding —
  together they suggest AI-exposed career *edges* (both entry and late-career) are fraying while the
  middle holds, which is a sharper "who bears the cost" story than either alone. It also **corrects**
  the anecdote-driven framing this project should be wary of: press coverage (WSJ's "Workers Opt to
  Retire Amid Shift to AI," cited in the brief itself) suggested *voluntary* retreat from AI adoption
  pressure, but the hard CPS data shows the rise is specifically in the **unemployment** (involuntary)
  channel, not retirement — i.e. it looks more like *displacement* than *opt-out*. This strengthens
  the mortality/cognitive-decline stakes already anchored (Kouchekinia et al., involuntary job loss →
  ~2 yrs' cognitive ageing, concentrated in men 51–64; Munnell/Rutledge/Sanzenbacher 2019, involuntary
  separation often forces early retirement) by supplying the missing "is this happening to older
  AI-exposed workers now" data point. → **candidate for evidence_base** (pairs with the Stanford/ADP
  young-cohort 🟢 anchor and the Kouchekinia cognitive-decline 🟢 anchor under a new "who bears the AI
  labour cost — the edges of the career, not the middle" framing).
  https://crr.bc.edu/are-the-careers-of-older-workers-being-cut-short-by-ai/
