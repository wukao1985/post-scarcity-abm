## Items

- **"AI Doesn't Reduce Work — It Intensifies It"** — Aruna Ranganathan & Xingqi Maggie Ye;
  *Harvard Business Review*, February 2026. https://hbr.org/2026/02/ai-doesnt-reduce-work-it-intensifies-it
  🟡 (longitudinal workplace study, N=200, 8 months, single US tech company; HBR is a
  practitioner venue, not a peer-reviewed academic journal; no control group; results apply to
  knowledge workers in a tech context, not the full workforce).
  *What it actually shows:* 83% of employees reported AI *increased* their workload. Mechanism:
  AI expanded individual task scope (product managers began writing code; researchers took on
  engineering tasks), eroded work/personal-time boundaries, and produced "workload creep"
  toward 12-hour days — without explicit managerial pressure; workers expanded voluntarily
  because the tool made it feel possible. Outcomes: cognitive fatigue, burnout signals, weaker
  decision-making. *Limits (state honestly):* single company, single sector (tech), not
  peer-reviewed, no comparison group, self-reported workload perception; the "intensification"
  partly reflects voluntary scope expansion, which may represent ambition rather than burden; the
  tech sector is anomalous in degree of AI adoption; cannot generalise to lower-exposure sectors.
  *Why it matters:* pairs directly with the BCG June 3 finding (logged June 8) that 66% of
  AI-freed workers get no guidance on redirecting saved time — together they sketch a picture
  where AI neither frees time meaningfully nor reduces cognitive burden; instead, it raises
  throughput expectations without redesigning roles. This is the micro-mechanism behind Ranjit
  et al.'s "automating the joy out of work" concern: the immediate lived experience for many
  knowledge workers is not richer work but *more* work, with expanded cognitive load. The
  "freed capacity → meaningful capacity" transition the project's policy chapter assumes is
  actively resisted by this dynamic.
  → idea: pairs with "the ungiven hours" (June 8 log) — the intensification finding is *why*
  freed hours don't materialise: when AI enables more output, organisational norms silently
  expand to absorb it, turning "time saved" into "throughput demanded."

- **"Pain in the Nation 2026: The Epidemics of Alcohol, Drug, and Suicide Deaths"** — Trust
  for America's Health (TFAH) & Well Being Trust; published May/June 2026; underlying data
  from National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). https://www.tfah.org/report-details/pain-in-the-nation-2026-report/
  🟢 (based on official NCHS mortality statistics; descriptive epidemiology; no causal
  attribution claimed or warranted for any single factor including AI).
  *What it actually shows:* deaths of despair (suicide + drug overdose + alcohol mortality)
  declined **16% overall in 2024 vs 2023** — the first significant national decrease since
  1999. Breakdown: drug overdose −26%, alcohol mortality −4%, suicide −3%. The report states
  the decline was present across all demographic and geographic groups. Regional persistence:
  Appalachia still runs ~33% above the national rate. Structural risk flag: SAMHSA lost ~$1B
  in grant funding in early 2026, threatening medication-assisted treatment, data collection,
  and prevention infrastructure. *Limits (state honestly):* the 2024 NCHS data does not
  establish cause — the decline is multi-factorial, most plausibly reflecting increased
  naloxone access, fentanyl-awareness campaigns, and treatment uptake from prior opioid-policy
  cycles; it is NOT attributable to any improvement in the work-and-meaning structural
  conditions Case & Deaton identified; the improvement may not continue given the SAMHSA
  funding cuts; Appalachian and low-income disparities persist; this is a point estimate for
  2024 and preliminary 2025 trends are not yet in this report.
  *Why it matters:* the evidence base carries Case & Deaton's deaths of despair as the 🟢
  floor for "what the stakes look like when work and meaning erode." This 2024 update is an
  important honest nuance: the monotonically-worsening reading is no longer accurate. The
  deaths-of-despair mortality trend has *partially reversed* — but for reasons (opioid
  policy, naloxone access) that do not address the underlying conditions Case & Deaton named.
  For the writing, this needs to be cited carefully: the stakes argument stands (Appalachian
  persistence, structural economic risk, the funding-cut threat), but the framing of an
  unbroken worsening trend is now out of date. The honest version: the crisis peaked around
  2021, declined in 2024, but the structural drivers are unresolved and the policy
  infrastructure enabling the decline is now under pressure.
  → candidate for evidence_base update: add 2024 nuance to the deaths of despair entry
  (Case & Deaton section) — first decline since 1999, opioid-policy driven, structural
  meaning/work conditions not addressed; cite TFAH 2026 with NCHS provenance.
