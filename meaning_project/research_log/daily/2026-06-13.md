## Items

- **"Building Pro-Worker Artificial Intelligence"** — Daron Acemoglu, David H. Autor &
  Simon Johnson; NBER Working Paper 34854 / The Hamilton Project at Brookings, February 2026.
  https://www.nber.org/papers/w34854
  🔴 (normative policy paper with a 🟡 conceptual taxonomy; not primary empirical data;
  produced for the Hamilton Project, a Brookings-affiliated policy initiative; the authors
  are among the most influential economists on AI and labour, but this paper is advocacy +
  framework, not a study with results).
  *What it actually shows:* A conceptual taxonomy distinguishing five modes of technological
  change — (1) labor-augmenting, (2) capital-augmenting, (3) automating, (4) expertise-
  leveling, and (5) new-task-creating — and argues that **only the last category is
  unambiguously pro-worker** in the long run. Expertise-leveling (AI democratising access to
  high-skill knowledge — e.g. a nurse getting access to diagnostic reasoning) appears
  beneficial, but the authors argue it can ultimately commodify the skills it democratises,
  following the same trajectory as earlier waves of labor-saving technology. Current AI
  deployment concentrates in categories 3–4, which they attribute to market failures:
  misaligned developer incentives, path-dependence in research trajectories, and a dominant
  "pro-automation ideology" among funders. Johnson: "We are not currently on the pro-worker
  AI path — and that's unfortunate, it's regrettable, it's avoidable." Nine policy
  directions are proposed (tax reform, investment in health/education AI, IP protections for
  worker expertise, antitrust). *Limits (state honestly):* This is a Hamilton Project policy
  paper — the five-category taxonomy is a conceptual lens, not tested against labour data
  here; the market-failure diagnosis is an argument, not a measurement; the paper does not
  address psychological or meaning dimensions of work at all, only wages and employment; the
  normative prescription ("we should build pro-worker AI") is clearly motivated reasoning
  from economists with a prior commitment to distributional concerns. *Why it matters for
  the meaning project:* The expertise-leveling / new-task-creating distinction is the most
  precise articulation yet of the "which kind of AI is actually pro-worker" question that
  the augmentation/automation watchlist tracks. The paper's key 🟡 implication — that
  democratising expertise may ultimately harm the workers it appears to help by commodifying
  previously scarce skills — directly bears on Q5/Q6 and the augmentation-vs-automation
  dual (economic + psychological) breakpoint. The market-failure argument is also the
  clearest statement yet for *why* the writing should not assume markets will naturally
  produce the meaning-preserving outcome. And the paper says nothing about purpose, identity,
  or psychological wellbeing — its income-and-employment frame is the same gap the project
  fills relative to Amodei (see June 12). Use as 🔴 policy-discourse context only.
  → idea: "expertise-leveling as a false friend" — the Acemoglu/Autor/Johnson argument
  that AI democratising expert knowledge may commodify the very skills it appears to support
  has a direct psychological counterpart: if competence satisfaction (SDT) depends on scarce
  expertise, leveling expertise may erode competence-based meaning even while nominally
  augmenting workers.
