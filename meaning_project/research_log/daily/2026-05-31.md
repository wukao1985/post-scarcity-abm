## Items

- **"Labor Market Impacts of AI: A New Measure and Early Evidence"** — Massenkoff & McCrory,
  Anthropic (5 Mar 2026). https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts — 🟢 (empirical,
  single-platform). Builds an **"observed exposure"** score from actual Claude usage that
  distinguishes **automation** (model performs the task) from **augmentation** (model assists a
  human). Key findings: (1) augmentation leads automation ~4:1 in current usage; (2) no detectable
  macro unemployment increase for high-exposure workers; (3) tentative ~hiring/job-finding slowdown
  for workers aged 22–25 in exposed occupations post-2023, barely significant and cautioned by the
  authors; cannot detect differential effects smaller than ~1pp. Highest observed exposure:
  computer programmers (0.75), customer service reps, data entry keyers (0.67). *Shows:* AI usage is
  real and skews higher-educated/higher-paid, but macro disruption is not yet measurable — an early
  signal, not a verdict. → candidate for evidence_base (first paper using actual usage, not just
  theoretical occupational-exposure scores). → watchlist variable: the automation/augmentation split
  — if it flips toward automation, that is the signal the meaning picture changes.
  → directly used 2026-05-31 in the contract-buffer occupation scan (see `contract_buffer_study/`):
  observed_exposure reproduces the customer-service entry-rung decline; programmers' employment grew
  (augmentation), consistent with this paper.

- **Lee et al., "Relying on AI at work reduces self-efficacy, ownership, and meaning while active
  collaboration mitigates the effects"** — *Scientific Reports* 16:13583 (2026).
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-42312-6 — 🟢 (pre-registered). Experiment (N=269) +
  follow-up (N=270): **passive** AI use (copy the model's output) reliably undermined self-efficacy,
  psychological ownership, and perceived work meaningfulness — and the declines **persisted** after
  returning to manual work; **active** collaboration (draft first, then AI-refine) preserved all
  three at levels indistinguishable from independent work. *Shows:* AI use *per se* doesn't corrode
  the psychological sources of meaning — *how* you use it does (passive consumption corrodes, active
  collaboration doesn't). → strong candidate for evidence_base; pairs with the SO study and the
  care/relatedness findings. → idea: "how you use AI" may matter more for meaning than "whether AI
  does your job."

- **"Do Job Postings Show Early Labor-Market Effects of AI?"** — NY Fed / Liberty Street Economics,
  14 May 2026.
  https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/
  — 🟢 (empirical, public). Occupational AI-exposure scores vs US vacancy data: <10% of
  workers/vacancies are in high-exposure occupations (≥0.4); ~40% are in zero-exposure jobs.
  Postings for AI-exposed occupations declined relative to others, **but the divergence began before
  ChatGPT (late 2022) and stabilised after 2023**, undercutting a clean AI-causation story; no
  specific entry-level concentration in postings. *Limit:* exposure = potential, not realised. →
  pairs with the contract-buffer study's null: both say the macro AI labour effect is not yet
  cleanly visible.

- **"Research on AI and the Labor Market Is Still in the First Inning"** — Jed Kolko, PIIE (2026,
  exact date unverified).
  https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/research-ai-and-labor-market-still-first-inning
  — 🟡 (commentary, not primary data). Reviews conflicting studies: Brynjolfsson et al. (young
  exposed workers hit harder) vs Eckhardt & Goldschlag (unemployment rose *less* in exposed
  occupations) — disagreement on which jobs are "exposed." Notes posting declines in exposed roles
  began in 2022, before ChatGPT (rate-cycle confound), and a "streetlamp bias" — research clusters
  where data is, not where effects are largest. *Why it matters:* honest framing for the economics
  layer — no clean identification of AI's net labour effect yet.

- **AI-exposed jobs aren't seeing faster unemployment** — Yale Budget Lab / multiple aggregation,
  May 2026 — 🟡 (mixed primary/secondary). Over the first ~30 months post-ChatGPT, no clear
  divergence in unemployment between high- and low-exposure occupations at the macro level. *Limit:*
  aggregate; may mask cohort/entry effects (cf. Stanford ADP young-worker finding). → tension to
  hold, not resolve.
