## Items

- **"Labor Market Impacts of AI: A New Measure and Early Evidence"** — Massenkoff & McCrory,
  Anthropic (5 Mar 2026). https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts — 🟢 (empirical,
  single-platform). Builds an **"observed exposure"** score from actual Claude usage that separates
  **automation** (model does the task) from **augmentation** (model assists a human). (1) augmentation
  leads automation ~4:1 in current usage; (2) no detectable macro unemployment rise for high-exposure
  workers; (3) tentative ~hiring/job-finding slowdown for ages 22–25 in exposed occupations post-2023,
  barely significant and cautioned; cannot detect differential effects <~1pp. Highest observed
  exposure: computer programmers (0.75), customer service reps, data entry keyers (0.67). *Shows:* AI
  usage is real, skews higher-educated/higher-paid, but macro disruption not yet measurable — early
  signal, not verdict. → evidence_base candidate (first paper using actual usage, not theoretical
  exposure scores). → watchlist: the automation/augmentation split — if it tips toward automation in
  cognitive work, that's the breakpoint. **Used directly 2026-05-31** in the contract-buffer
  occupation scan (`contract_buffer_study/`): observed_exposure reproduces a customer-service
  entry-rung employment decline, while software developers' employment grew (augmentation) — consistent
  with this paper.

- **Lee et al., "Relying on AI at work reduces self-efficacy, ownership, and meaning while active
  collaboration mitigates the effects"** — *Scientific Reports* 16:13583 (2026).
  https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-42312-6 — 🟢 (pre-registered). Experiment (N=269) +
  follow-up (N=270): **passive** AI use (copy the output) reliably undermined self-efficacy,
  psychological ownership, and perceived work meaningfulness — and the declines **persisted** after
  returning to manual work; **active** collaboration (draft first, then AI-refine) preserved all three
  at levels indistinguishable from independent work. *Shows:* AI use *per se* doesn't corrode the
  psychological sources of meaning — *how* you use it does. → strong evidence_base candidate; pairs
  with the SO study and the care/relatedness findings. → idea: augmentation-vs-automation is also a
  *psychological* category, not only an economic one.

- **"Do Job Postings Show Early Labor-Market Effects of AI?"** — Audoly, Guerin & Topa, NY Fed /
  Liberty Street Economics, 14 May 2026.
  https://libertystreeteconomics.newyorkfed.org/2026/05/do-job-postings-show-early-labor-market-effects-of-ai/
  — 🟢 (empirical, public). Lightcast vacancies × occupational AI-exposure: postings for high-exposure
  occupations did decline relative to others, but the divergence **predates ChatGPT** and tracks the
  2022 rate cycle better than the LLM launch; no within-occupation junior-vs-senior split; <10% of
  workers in high-exposure occupations, ~40% in zero-exposure. *Limit:* exposure = potential, not
  realised. → pairs with the contract-buffer null: the macro AI labour effect is not yet cleanly
  visible, and attribution is confounded with the rate cycle.

- **"A reality check on the AI jobs hysteria"** — MIT Technology Review, 26 May 2026.
  https://www.technologyreview.com/2026/05/26/1137855/a-reality-check-on-the-ai-jobs-hysteria/ — 🟡
  (journalism wrapping 🟢 primary findings). Synthesises: Stanford Digital Economy Lab (ADP payroll,
  950 occs) — **~16% drop in entry-level jobs for ages 22–25 in AI-exposed fields since ~late 2022,
  while older workers in the same roles grew**; BLS — AI-exposed occupations currently have *lower*
  unemployment than less-exposed ones; Fed — coder employment growth slowed ~3% but still positive.
  Net: no aggregate disruption yet, but a real young-cohort signal at the bottom of the career ladder.
  Note the tension with the NY Fed item (postings slump not cleanly AI-attributable vs ADP's specific
  young-cohort payroll drop) — both can hold (vacancies ≠ headcount). → idea: the career-ladder bottom
  rung as the first place post-labour meaning-loss would appear.

- **"Research on AI and the Labor Market Is Still in the First Inning"** — Jed Kolko, PIIE (2026,
  exact date unverified).
  https://www.piie.com/blogs/realtime-economics/2026/research-ai-and-labor-market-still-first-inning
  — 🟡 (commentary). Conflicting studies (Brynjolfsson: young exposed hit harder; Eckhardt &
  Goldschlag: unemployment rose *less* in exposed occs); posting declines began pre-ChatGPT (rate
  confound); "streetlamp bias" — research clusters where data is, not where effects are largest.
  *Why it matters:* no clean identification of AI's net labour effect yet; calibrate certainty.

- **"Crashing Waves vs. Rising Tides: Preliminary Findings on AI Automation from Thousands of Worker
  Evaluations"** — arXiv 2604.01363 (Apr 2026) — 🟡 (preliminary, projection-heavy). 17,000+ worker
  evaluations over 3,000+ O*NET text tasks: AI success on ~3–4h text tasks rose ~50% (Q2 2024) → ~65%
  (Q3 2025), a "rising tide" of continuous improvement; projects 80–95% by 2029 if the slope holds.
  *Caveats:* text tasks only; linear extrapolation. *Why it matters:* if it holds, the gap between
  "AI can" and "AI is doing it at scale" narrows — the adjustment-speed analogy-breakpoint gets more
  urgent. (Verify the arXiv id from primary before any reuse.)

## Watchlist movement
- **Automation vs augmentation (analogy breakpoint #1):** still augmentation-dominant (~4:1, Anthropic).
  The clearest *employment* signal sits where usage is automation-type (customer-service entry rung),
  not where usage is highest-but-augmentative (software developers, still growing). Flag if the split
  tips toward automation in cognitive occupations.
- **Speed vs adjustment (#2):** "Crashing Waves" suggests capability is outrunning the (slow) empirical
  labour research; impact may trail capability by years.
- **The "new jobs" mechanism (#3):** flagged by Kolko as understudied; IMF SDN/2026/001 ("Bridging
  Skill Gaps", Jan 2026) reportedly finds ~1 in 10 advanced-economy vacancies now needs an AI/IT skill
  with a wage premium but deepened polarisation — **flagged for the weekly digest, not yet read in
  primary.**
