## Items

- **Bohmann, Fiedler, Kasy, Schupp & Schwerter, "Cash Transfers, Mental Health, and Agency:
  Evidence from an RCT in Germany"** — CESifo Working Paper No. 11989 (this version 23 Mar
  2026; original version Jul 2025); verified from the primary PDF (ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11989.pdf).
  🟢 (same pre-registered RCT already anchored in evidence_base — €1,200/mo for 3 yr, N=107
  treated/1,580 control, AEA RCT Registry #7734 — but this version adds a genuinely new wave of
  results not previously captured here). **What it shows, precisely:** the trial added two
  post-program follow-up waves (6 and 18 months after the final transfer, i.e. through ~Nov
  2025). The mental-health-and-wellbeing (MHW) index improved **0.379 SD during the 3-year
  program**, **0.331 SD six months after** transfers stopped, and **0.362 SD eighteen months
  after** — i.e. **91% of the during-program effect persisted after the money stopped flowing**.
  Individual components tell a sharper story: **income satisfaction fades** (0.68 SD at 6mo into
  the program → 0.35 SD by program's end → 0.13 SD at 18mo post-program, no longer significant)
  — ordinary hedonic adaptation to the lost income stream — while **mental health, general life
  satisfaction, health satisfaction, sleep satisfaction, and purpose in life hold at 0.28–0.31 SD
  even 18 months after the payments ended**, closely tracking their in-program magnitudes. The
  authors' own account: cash transfers enabled durable life changes (participants saved ~1/3 of
  the transfer, gave 7-8% to others, gained 1.3+ hrs/week with friends, slept longer, reported
  higher perceived autonomy [+0.32 SD]) that became self-sustaining once established, rather
  than a purely mechanical effect of the extra income. **Why it matters for the project:** this
  is the sharpest evidence yet against reading cash-transfer wellbeing gains as merely "relief
  while the money flows" — it directly deepens the evidence_base's core UBI synthesis, which
  already flagged the German RCT as a "stable, no-fade" counterweight to the Altman/OpenResearch
  fade but only covered the *during-program* period. The new post-program persistence is a
  materially different and stronger claim: *durability after the transfer ends*, not just
  stability *while* it runs — and it explicitly contrasts with the contemporaneous US RCT
  (Miller/Vivalt/Bartik 2024, USD 1,000/mo to multi-person households), which found no durable
  mental-health effect at all. The paper's own explanation for the divergence — individual
  (not household-shared) discretionary control over the transfer as the operative mechanism —
  is a testable, falsifiable claim worth tracking against future high-income-country pilots.
  **Limits (state honestly):** still N=107 treated (small), single country, self-report
  outcomes, non-representative sample (single-person households, below-average income, age
  21-40), and the "self-sustaining life changes" mechanism is described as *exploratory*, not
  itself causally identified. This is a revision of an already-partially-anchored paper, not a
  brand-new study — flagged here because the post-program persistence finding was not captured
  in the evidence_base entry (which covered only "stable across all three years") or in any
  daily log since Jul–Aug 2026 checks that reported "no new UBI/guaranteed-income primary result
  since the German RCT." → **candidate for evidence_base update** (extend the existing German
  RCT entry with the 18-month post-program persistence figures). → idea: this is the cleanest
  empirical anchor yet for a piece on *why* some cash-transfer effects on meaning outlast the
  money and others don't — individual-vs-household discretionary control as the hinge variable.
  https://www.ifo.de/DocDL/cesifo1_wp11989.pdf
