## Items

- **"Labor Market Impacts of AI: A New Measure and Early Evidence"** — Maxim Massenkoff &
  Peter McCrory; Anthropic Research, March 5, 2026.
  https://www.anthropic.com/research/labor-market-impacts
  🟢 (novel observed-exposure methodology; real usage data rather than theoretical task
  scoring) — **with a prominent caveat: this is an Anthropic-authored paper using Anthropic's
  own Claude usage data as its exposure measure. Provider conflict of interest is real and
  cannot be dismissed. The findings should be treated as suggestive, not settled, pending
  replication with independent data sources.**
  *What it actually shows:* Introduces "observed exposure" — a measure combining occupational
  task taxonomies with which tasks are *actually being automated* in practice (derived from
  Claude.ai and API usage logs), rather than only what AI *could theoretically* do. Main
  empirical findings: (a) **No systematic unemployment increase** for workers in the most
  AI-exposed occupations since November 2022 — the unemployment null holds; (b) **Suggestive
  evidence of a hiring-rate slowdown**: the rate at which younger workers *enter* high-exposure
  occupations has narrowed since ~2024, even while the unemployment stock of workers already
  in those roles appears stable; (c) Computer programmers (~75% observed task coverage), data
  entry keyers (~67%), and customer service reps (~65%) rank as most exposed on this measure.
  The paper names a "Great Recession for white-collar workers" as a detectable scenario (would
  require roughly doubling from ~3% to ~6% unemployment in the top-exposure quartile) and
  states it has not yet occurred but could. *Limits (state honestly):* Single-provider data
  (Anthropic's Claude) for the exposure measure introduces selection bias — Claude usage may
  not represent the full LLM deployment landscape; the paper is authored by Anthropic employees,
  creating an obvious interest in findings that are neither alarmist nor dismissive; the hiring-
  slowdown finding is described as "suggestive" in secondary coverage; the window (Nov 2022–early
  2026) is still short; the unemployment null is consistent with other independent studies
  (contract-buffer null, NY Fed) but those also have their own limits. The "observed vs.
  feasible exposure" gap (actual adoption = small fraction of what AI *could* automate) is an
  important methodological contribution but also underscores that the early-window null does
  not rule out larger future effects. *Why it matters:* The "observed exposure" framework is a
  genuine methodological advance over purely theoretical task-scoring (Felten; Eloundou et al.)
  because it captures actual deployment rather than capability. The key substantive finding for
  the project is the *dual* signal: unemployment stock is calm, but the *flow* signal (entry
  into high-exposure roles) is already narrowing. This is the mechanism by which the Stanford/
  ADP entry-level employment drop and the NY Fed remote-work confound could ultimately resolve
  toward an AI-attribution: if the door into exposed roles is narrowing, the stock will follow
  with a lag. The project's "early innings / loop not yet closing" read holds, but this adds a
  more specific early-warning indicator.
  → candidate for evidence_base (observed-exposure methodology; hiring-flow narrowing as
  leading indicator preceding unemployment stock signal; pairs with Stanford/ADP young-cohort
  finding and NY Fed confound).

- **"Does Employment Slow Cognitive Decline? Evidence from Labor Market Shocks"** — Noah Arman
  Kouchekinia, David Neumark & Tim A. Bruckner; NBER Working Paper 35117, April 2026 (revised
  May 2026). https://www.nber.org/papers/w35117
  🟢 (causal identification via Bartik-style IV using local labor demand shocks; HRS data;
  not pre-registered as far as known; concentrated finding, significant caveats).
  *What it actually shows:* Uses the Health and Retirement Study (HRS) for US adults, exploiting
  plausibly exogenous shifts in local labor demand (Bartik instrument) to estimate the causal
  effect of involuntary employment loss on cognitive outcomes. Main finding: **negative local
  labor demand shocks that push workers out of employment are causally associated with
  accelerated cognitive decline**, concentrated among **men aged 51–64**; for affected workers,
  the estimated cognitive score drop is equivalent to approximately **2 extra years of normal
  age-related decline**. The effect runs through the employment channel specifically (not through
  income alone), and the study extends beyond the formal retirement-age window to cover the
  pre-retirement period. *Limits (state honestly):* The Bartik instrument validity is debated
  in the literature — local demand shocks affect workers via multiple channels beyond just
  employment (local amenities, stress, health insurance access), so the IV may not isolate
  employment cleanly; HRS sample skews older (51+) and the cognitive findings are not shown
  for younger or middle-career cohorts; effects are concentrated in men and may not generalise
  to women or non-binary workers; data predates the generative-AI era — the path from "AI as
  a labor demand shock" to "accelerated cognitive decline" is an *inference*, not measured
  directly in this paper; the 2-year estimate is an approximation from secondary coverage
  (verify from primary before citing the specific figure). *Why it matters:* The project
  already holds two 🟢 anchors for the "stakes are serious" argument: Case & Deaton's deaths
  of despair (structural work erosion → mortality) and Emile's (2026) finding that purpose
  erosion mediates loneliness→mortality (88% mediation). This paper adds a third dimension:
  **involuntary employment removal causally accelerates cognitive aging.** If AI-driven
  displacement operates as a local labor demand shock (plausible for occupationally-concentrated
  job losses), the implication is not only lost income and lost meaning, but measurable
  neurological harm — the equivalent of ageing ~2 years faster. The finding is concentrated in
  men 51–64, precisely the demographic that overlaps with mid-career workers in the most-exposed
  white-collar occupations (insurance, banking, information processing — the sectors already
  declining in BLS data). The honest framing: AI displacement MAY produce cognitive consequences
  via the same mechanism as other labor shocks, but this has not been directly tested; what IS
  established is that involuntary job loss in this age-group is cognitively costly and the
  effect is not trivially explained by income alone. Pairs with the retirement & wellbeing
  literature and strengthens the project's case that "just find meaning elsewhere" responses
  to displacement underestimate the full cognitive and neurological stakes.
  → candidate for evidence_base (cognitive/neurological consequences of involuntary employment
  removal; Bartik IV; pairs with Emile purpose→mortality and retirement literature).
  → idea: "the cognitive stakes" — the harm from AI displacement isn't only income loss or
  meaning loss; there may be neurological consequences if mid-career displacement operates
  as a de facto early-retirement shock; the "find purpose elsewhere" framing underestimates
  how cognitively protective structured work is, beyond its psychological functions.
