## Items

- **BLS Employment Situation — May 2026** — U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics (released
  June 5, 2026). https://www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.htm
  🟢 (official government payroll and household survey data; sector declines not
  AI-attributable on their own — BLS assigns no cause).
  *What it actually shows:* +172,000 nonfarm payrolls in May (beat consensus estimate of
  ~85K); unemployment rate unchanged at 4.3% (held in a narrow 4.3–4.5% band since
  July 2025). Sector detail relevant to AI exposure: **financial activities −22,000 in
  May, down 107,000 since its peak in May 2025**, with losses concentrated in insurance
  carriers (−11K) and commercial banking (−3K) — the exact role categories Goldman Sachs
  flagged as most-substituted in its April 2026 payroll decomposition (logged June 4).
  **Information sector down 342,000 (−11%) since its November 2022 peak**, with computing
  infrastructure providers, data processing, and web hosting each declining in May. Growth
  sectors: leisure/hospitality, local government, healthcare (+35K in line with 12-month
  average). Wage growth: +3.4% YoY.
  *Limits (state honestly):* BLS does not attribute sector-level changes to AI;
  financial-activities and information declines may reflect the 2022–23 tech-cycle
  correction, higher rates, or corporate restructuring — not AI alone. The November 2022
  alignment with the ChatGPT launch is suggestive but not causal. The headline (+172K,
  4.3% U) cannot be decomposed into AI vs. non-AI effects from public BLS data.
  *Why it matters:* the macro picture is consistent with prior logs' "no aggregate
  disruption yet" reading — the Goldman Sachs net-~11K/month AI substitution estimate
  remains small relative to overall payroll growth. But the sector pattern is beginning
  to cohere: financial activities (insurance, banking) and information (data processing)
  — two of the highest AI-exposure sectors — show persistent multi-month declines, while
  the headline gains come from lower-AI-exposure sectors (hospitality, health, local
  government). The growth sectors are not re-employing the workers displaced from the
  declining ones — this is consistent with the IMF SDN/2026/001 finding (new-jobs
  mechanism delivers headcount in non-AI sectors) and the Goldman Sachs "new jobs =
  construction, not re-employed admin workers" observation. The most honest summary:
  the BLS data does not yet prove AI is causing sector-level losses, but it is no longer
  inconsistent with that reading either.
  → watchlist: track financial activities and information sector monthly — if declines
  persist/accelerate as construction-sector buffer plateaus (see Goldman June 4 note),
  the GS gross-substitution estimate will start showing up in public sector data.

- **NACEWEB 2026 Job Outlook Spring Update — "Demand for AI Skills in Entry-level Jobs
  Nearly Triples Since Fall 2025"** — National Association of Colleges and Employers
  (survey conducted Feb 12–Mar 17, 2026; published spring 2026, not strictly 24–48h
  fresh). https://www.naceweb.org/job-market/trends-and-predictions/demand-for-ai-skills-in-entry-level-jobs-nearly-triples-since-fall-2025
  🟢 (employer survey; N=185, 142 NACE employer members; self-reported intent; not
  pre-registered; single survey wave).
  *What it actually shows:* more than one-third of entry-level job postings now require
  AI skills — nearly triple the share from the fall 2025 survey (two waves prior). 28%
  of employers specifically seek AI-capable early-career hires; ~60% are already assigning
  interns AI-tool projects. Separately: **40% of early-career workers report they have
  changed or are considering changing their career plans because of AI.** *Limits (state
  honestly):* self-report employer survey (N=185; low-N for a national claim); "require
  AI skills" is employer-defined and potentially loose — watch for whether the tripling
  reflects genuine job redesign or credential inflation (adding AI requirements to
  unchanged roles); the 40% early-career figure is the most striking number but is a
  single-wave survey without controls for framing effects or comparison group. Not
  causal.
  *Why it matters:* the 40% career-plan-change figure is the most meaning-adjacent
  number in this report — not a direct measure of wellbeing or purpose, but career
  identity disruption reported at scale by young workers. It pairs with the Stanford/ADP
  employment decline (young cohort already in evidence_base), the OECD double-exposure
  framing (young + loneliness, logged June 2), and the Ranjit et al. task-meaning paper
  (logged June 3). The speed of skills-demand shift (tripling in roughly two survey
  quarters) is the most concrete data point yet for the adjustment-speed watchlist: if
  the skills goalposts shift quarterly, no retraining pipeline — which operates on 1–4
  year cycles — can track them. This complements and sharpens the cautionary 🟢 on
  retraining failure already in evidence_base (WIA/WIOA, TAA evaluations).
  → idea: "the moving goalposts" — piece on how the quarterly skills-churn rate makes
  the standard "just retrain" policy prescription structurally impossible before one even
  debates whether retraining works, and what that means for policy and for individuals
  trying to anchor meaning to occupational identity.

## Watchlist movement

- **Substitution vs. augmentation (analogy breakpoint #1):** BLS May sector detail adds
  texture: financial activities (−107K in 12 months) and information (−342K since Nov
  2022) are declining persistently while aggregate employment grows. Pattern is consistent
  with Goldman Sachs gross-substitution reading from June 4, but the headline remains
  stable — macro disruption signal absent. Growth sectors (healthcare, hospitality, local
  government) are lower-AI-exposure: the net jobs being created are not in AI-exposed
  roles. Consistent with the IMF SDN/2026/001 reading.
- **Speed vs. adjustment (#2):** NACEWEB skills-demand tripling in ~two survey quarters
  is the fastest skills-churn signal logged so far. The adjustment-speed breakpoint
  tightens: even if retraining programmes worked (they mostly don't — see evidence_base),
  quarterly goalposts make individual adjustment structurally harder.
- **The "new jobs" mechanism (#3):** BLS sector gains (hospitality, healthcare, local
  gov) confirm the pattern: the mechanism is producing new jobs, but they are in
  non-AI-exposed, typically lower-wage sectors. The loop is not closing for displaced
  finance/information workers.
