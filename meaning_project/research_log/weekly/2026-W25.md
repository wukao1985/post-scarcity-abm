## The week in one paragraph

A **mechanism-sharpening week, not a picture-changing one.** On the contested **labour-attribution**
front nothing moved: the fog (NY Fed remote-work confound, NY Fed postings null, contract-buffer
null, Massenkoff & McCrory hiring-flow narrowing, Yale Budget Lab null) sat exactly where W23–W24
left it, and the week's new labour item — **PwC's Global AI Jobs Barometer** — is a conflicted 🟡
that reframes rather than resolves: entry-level roles aren't vanishing, they're being *re-specified*
upward (AI-exposed entry roles grew 35% since 2019 but now demand **7× more senior-level skills**),
which I'm logging as **"the raised floor"** — a refinement of *experience creep* from "door closed"
to "bottom rung lifted out of reach." But the week's real substance is on the **meaning** side, and
it *sharpens* rather than adds. The standout is **Yoneda et al. (JPSP, 175,000 adults, 18 countries,
harmonized coordinated analysis)**: when you statistically separate them, it is **subjective
loneliness (the *feeling* of disconnection), not objective social isolation (lack of contact)**, that
predicts cognitive impairment and shorter life — isolation showed *no consistent* cognitive link.
This closes a loop the project had left open: AI companions reduce *isolation* (you are "talking to
someone") while leaving *loneliness* unchanged or worse (Folk & Dunn, Aalto, Li et al.) — so they
**target the wrong variable**, and the wrong variable is the one with the serious health
consequences. Tellingly, Yoneda's team recommends treating loneliness as a clinical **"vital
sign"** — independently echoing the project's own coined "Purpose is a vital sign," so *both* limbs
of the meaning case (purpose and relatedness) are now framed by outside researchers as measurable
health variables, not soft concerns. Folded from Monday's scan, **Chueri et al. (Perspectives on
Politics)** supplies the demand-side mirror: when people fear automation they do **not** primarily
ask for more unemployment benefits — they ask for measures that **preserve work's social role**
(robot taxes, income-regardless-of-employment). That is the public, in survey data, independently
affirming the project's **economic–meaning decoupling** — Jahoda's latent functions showing up as a
*political preference*. The throughline: the layer the world argues about (jobs) stood still this
week; the layer this project owns (meaning) got *more precisely specified* — we now know which
variable in the loneliness story is pathogenic, and that the public already wants the role of work,
not just its income, protected.

## Notable items

- **"Loneliness — not social isolation — predicts cognitive impairment and shorter life"** —
  Tomiko Yoneda et al. (24-author international team; UC Davis-led); *Journal of Personality and
  Social Psychology*, 15 Jun 2026. 🟢 (175,000 adults aged 50+ across 18 countries; harmonized
  *coordinated-analysis* framework — multiple cohorts modelled in parallel, a strong replication
  design; observational; older-adult sample; loneliness self-reported; **primary text not retrieved
  — specifics from the UC Davis/UC press release and secondary coverage**). *Shows:* with loneliness
  and isolation mathematically separated, **subjective loneliness** consistently predicts higher risk
  of severe cognitive impairment (a 10% rise in reported loneliness ≈ 8–9% higher impairment risk)
  and shorter life, and reduces the chance of recovering from mild impairment by ~3%; **objective
  social isolation showed no consistent cognitive link** and only a weak mortality association. The
  authors' framing: "Loneliness is a perception… you could be surrounded by a crowd and still feel
  lonely." They recommend screening loneliness as a clinical **"vital sign."** *Significance:* the
  most important item of the week, and a *mechanism* upgrade to the project's best-evidenced thread.
  It identifies the **pathogenic variable** — the felt experience, not the contact count — which
  closes the AI-companion loop: companions can cut *isolation* without touching *loneliness*, and
  loneliness is what carries the cognitive/mortality cost. Adds **cognitive decline** as a third
  health stake on the relatedness side (beside purpose-erosion and premature mortality), and pairs
  with Kouchekinia et al. (job loss → ~2 years' accelerated cognitive ageing): work supplies *both*
  structured contact *and* purpose, so its erosion threatens the same cognitive endpoint by two
  routes. https://www.ucdavis.edu/news/loneliness-drives-cognitive-impairment-and-shorter-life-more-social-isolation-new-study

- **PwC 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer** — PwC; released 15 Jun 2026; >1 billion job ads, 27
  countries. 🟡 (descriptive job-ad analysis from a firm that *sells* AI advisory — structural
  conflict of interest; proprietary, undisclosed methodology; job ads ≠ filled roles; no causal
  identification). *Shows:* AI-skill jobs grew 69% vs 9% market-wide; AI-skill wage premium up to
  62%. The meaning-relevant finding: **AI-exposed entry-level roles grew 35% since 2019 (non-exposed
  entry roles fell 10%) — but those entry roles are now 7× more likely to require traditionally
  senior skills** (judgment, leadership, creativity). PwC's "professionalised vs democratised"
  two-track split maps loosely onto the project's augmentation/automation axis. *Significance:*
  reframes *experience creep* — the entry-level problem may not be "the door is closed" (the NY Fed
  postings null finds no junior/senior vacancy divergence) but **"the floor of expected competence
  has been raised to a height that takes years to reach,"** disrupting the developmental arc by which
  early-career work builds SDT *competence*. Directionally interesting, but the source conflict is
  prominent and it sits in unresolved tension with the NY Fed and IMF findings; logged 🟡, not as a
  🟢 anchor. https://www.pwc.com/gx/en/news-room/press-releases/2026/pwc-2026-ai-jobs-barometer.html

- **"AI, the Future of Work, and the Politics of the Welfare State"** — Chueri, Gandenberger,
  Taylor, Knotz & Fossati; *Perspectives on Politics* (Cambridge), 28 Apr 2026 (folded from the
  Jun-22 scan). 🟡/🟢 (peer-reviewed political science; cross-national OECD "Risks that Matter" 2024
  attitudinal survey; observational, not causal; stated not revealed preferences; pre-dates the
  fullest post-ChatGPT debate; **primary text not retrieved — from Cambridge abstract + secondary**).
  *Shows:* fear of AI automation is widespread across educational groups, and it does **not** chiefly
  raise support for traditional income-support (unemployment benefits, retraining); it drives demand
  for measures that **preserve the social role of work** — robot taxes, guaranteed income *regardless
  of employment status*. *Significance:* the rare *political-science* 🟢-quality datum (not economics
  or psychology) and a demand-side mirror of the MANIFESTO's core claim. The public, asked, does not
  answer "just replace the income" — it implicitly recognises Jahoda's latent functions and wants
  work's *role* protected. Empirical legs for the **economic–meaning decoupling** and a counterweight
  to the techno-optimist "income support alone will do" reading; feeds Ch.7's 🔴 policy layer.
  https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/ai-the-future-of-work-and-the-politics-of-the-welfare-state/92E806B812B86A8944003B77038DCAA8

- **(Broader-sweep / flagged, low weight) Two ILO macro papers** — *"The Impact of GenAI on Jobs,
  Productivity and Work Organization: A Review of the Empirical Evidence"* (1 Jun 2026, 7 countries)
  and *"The Aggregation Paradox of AI"* (6 May 2026). 🟡 (institutional synthesis; **primary text
  not retrieved**; treat as indicative). *Show:* a consistent **micro-signal / macro-null** pattern —
  real but uneven, often unverified task-level productivity gains that have **not** yet translated
  into measured output, earnings, employment, or any official aggregate productivity signal; AI sits
  in the early phase of a **J-curve**, with the electrification/ICT precedent implying the aggregate
  payoff lags *organisational and institutional* adaptation by years-to-decades. *Significance:* both
  bear on the **speed-vs-adjustment** breakpoint and sharpen the project's framing — the live question
  is not "will AI ever pay off?" but **"what do people do for meaning *in the lag*?"** Both flagged
  for primary-text retrieval before any 🟢 use; logged here so future scans don't double-count them.

## Running threads

- **Relatedness (Q3) — the mechanism is now specified, not just the direction.** The
  multi-method convergence (Aalto quasi-exp + Folk & Dunn panel + Li et al. RCT + empathy-preference
  + Emile/OECD mortality) established that AI companionship fails to fix loneliness and may worsen it.
  Yoneda now tells us **why that matters and which variable is decisive**: it is *felt* loneliness,
  not *objective* isolation, that drives cognitive impairment and mortality — and companions move the
  latter, not the former. The project's best-evidenced sub-question gains a named pathogenic target.
  **Track:** any AI-companion study that measures *subjective loneliness* (not contact/usage) as the
  outcome; replication of the subjective/objective split in working-age samples; whether "bridge, not
  endpoint" companion designs can shift the *feeling*.
- **The entry rung — still contested; PwC reframes the shape.** No attribution movement. PwC's
  "raised floor" (entry roles survive but demand 7× senior skills) is a *third* shape for the same
  contested phenomenon — alongside "selectively closing" (Stanford/ADP, M&M flow) and "not
  AI-distinct at all" (NY Fed postings, remote-work confound). The honest read remains: the
  *phenomenon* (a worse early-career rung) is plausibly real; its *AI attribution* is the
  most-contested 🟢 in the project. **Track:** independent (non-commercial) replication of the
  "7× senior-skill demand" finding; the BLS occupational employment update.
- **The stakes — cognition consolidating as the third dimension.** Yoneda (loneliness → cognitive
  impairment) joins Kouchekinia (job loss → ~2 years' cognitive ageing): two independent routes from
  work-and-meaning erosion to the *same* neurological endpoint, since work supplies both structured
  contact and purpose. The "vital sign" framing (loneliness in Yoneda; purpose in Emile) recurs.
  **Track:** working-age replications; whether clinical/public-health bodies adopt loneliness/purpose
  screening.
- **Policy discourse — now with a demand-side data point.** W24's supply-side finding (Amodei +
  Acemoglu/Autor/Johnson stop at income/headcount) gains a demand-side counterpart in Chueri: the
  *public* already wants work's role preserved, not just its income replaced. The meaning-shaped hole
  in elite policy is *not* matched by a meaning-blind public. **Track:** whether any major proposal
  engages purpose/role-preservation; whether robot-tax / role-preservation preferences harden into
  political pressure.
- **Speed vs adjustment — the "lag" framing tightens.** The two ILO papers (micro-signal/macro-null,
  J-curve) reinforce that the aggregate payoff — and thus the fiscal/political room to fund
  meaning-sustaining institutions — lags by years-to-decades. **Track:** primary-text retrieval of
  both ILO papers; any official-statistics aggregate AI-productivity signal.

## Watchlist (the analogy breakpoints)

- **What is being automated (muscle → routine cognition → judgment/creativity)?** No new capability
  data this week. PwC's "raised floor" implies that within surviving entry roles the *routine* layer
  is absorbed while the *judgment* layer is demanded earlier — consistent with "routine cognition
  first," now visible as a re-specification of the job rather than its elimination. **Movement:**
  none on the frontier; one reframe on how absorption shows up at entry level.
- **Speed vs society's adjustment timescale?** The ILO J-curve / aggregation-paradox framing is the
  week's movement: real micro gains, invisible macro gains, institutional adaptation still pending —
  the adjustment lag is the binding constraint, and the meaning question lives *inside* it.
- **Does 'new jobs appear' still hold (or does AI do the new jobs too)?** No change. PwC's 35%
  entry-role growth is the optimistic reading, but its 7×-senior-skill caveat and undisclosed
  conflicted methodology keep it from settling the question; the broader-sweep corroboration
  (Revelio, UK tech grads) restates the worry without resolving attribution. **Movement:** none
  substantive.
