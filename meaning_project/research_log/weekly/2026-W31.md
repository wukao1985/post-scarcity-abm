## The week in one paragraph

**A genuinely quiet week whose single item sharpens *how the project cites*, not *what the project
knows*.** No jobs report landed in-window (the July Employment Situation is 7 Aug, W32); no new
occupation-level displacement study, no new UBI result, no new relatedness paper. The week's one
real item, logged Jul 28, is the **Chicago Fed's WP 2026-12** (Broady, Dunson & Barr) — a
descriptive, government-data analysis showing that *which exposure metric you pick can flip the sign
of the employment story on the very same occupations over the very same years*: on a usage-based
"AI applicability" measure, the most-exposed occupations *grew* employment +4.05% (2019–24); on Frey
& Osborne's classic *computerizability* risk score, high-risk occupations *fell* −3%. Same workers,
same window, opposite direction — and wages rose across every tier regardless. It is explicitly
**non-causal** (the authors say so; COVID sits inside the window; the high-applicability group is
only 38 occupations), so it is **not** an evidence_base anchor for any displacement claim. Its value
is a methodological one the project should absorb: a reminder that its own exposure-based anchors
(Stanford/ADP, ECB, Census BTOS, PwC) each smuggle in a *construct choice*, and that headlines cite
whichever construct flatters the story — precisely the "cite what the source shows, not the
headline" discipline this project runs on. Two retrieval-queue candidates also surfaced (both
flagged, neither new): the **MAGMA/Marienthal** Austrian job-guarantee natural experiment (a rare
test of Jahoda's latent functions isolated from income), and the **WashU/Abdalla & Galea**
eight-country young-adult loneliness study — both parked for a dedicated primary-source session, not
logged as fresh signals. Net: the story hasn't moved; the honest headline is the quiet itself, plus
one sharpening of the tools.

## Notable items

- **Broady, Dunson & Barr, "Rethinking Automation Risk: AI Applicability and Occupational Outcomes,
  2019–24"** — Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago Working Paper 2026-12 (published 2 Jul 2026; verified
  from the primary PDF, doi.org/10.21033/wp-2026-12; logged in the Jul 28 daily). 🟢 **descriptive,
  explicitly non-causal** (real BLS occupational employment/wage data + O*NET, 2019–2024; the authors
  state "these findings do not prove that automation or AI caused the observed employment or wage
  changes" and flag DiD as future work; COVID sits inside the window as a major confound; small N at
  the occupation level — the "high AI applicability" group is only 38 occupations). *Shows:* matching
  Tomlinson et al.'s (2025) Copilot-usage-based "AI applicability" scores to occupations, employment
  in the highest-applicability occupations *grew* **+4.05%** (335,800 jobs) and wages **+21.7%**,
  2019–24; but using Frey & Osborne's (2017) *computerization-risk* scores, employment *fell* on
  average in high-risk (**−3%**) and moderate-risk (**−6%**) occupations while growing in low-risk
  ones (+8%) — with wages rising **21–26% in all three risk tiers**, and within every tier the
  variance swamping the average (their "high risk" spans telemarketers at −51% to models at +131%).
  *Significance for the meaning niche:* a fresh, primary-sourced demonstration that **the
  augmentation-vs-automation employment story is metric-dependent** — usage-based exposure and
  task-based exposure point in *opposite* directions on the same occupations. It sharpens rather than
  resolves the augmentation/automation watchlist, and it is a direct, worked caution against citing
  any single exposure-score headline (including the project's own Stanford/ADP, ECB, Census BTOS
  citations) without naming *which construct is doing the work*. The CFO Dive coverage that surfaced
  it ("Jobs in occupations most exposed to AI increased by 4%") reported the applicability figure
  correctly but omitted the opposite-signed computerizability result in the same paper — the
  headline-vs-primary gap the project exists to catch. *Not an evidence_base anchor* (descriptive,
  single WP, COVID confound); logged as a **methodological caution** (see Feeds-in). → revisit if a
  causal (DiD) follow-up appears, per the authors' own stated next step.
