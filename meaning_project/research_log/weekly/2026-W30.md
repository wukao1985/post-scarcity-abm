## The week in one paragraph

**A quiet week whose two real items both deepen existing threads rather than open a new one — and that is
itself the honest headline.** After W29's "edges fray first" synthesis move, W30 produced no new
aggregate data and no new occupation-level displacement study; the underlying macro-null / selective-
concentration picture is exactly where W29 left it (next hard aggregate point: the July Employment
Situation, 7 Aug 2026). The week's genuine 🟢 is a **catch-up**, not a same-week release: Chi et al.,
*"Optimized but Unowned"* (arXiv, preregistered, N=470), verified this week from the primary — AI-authored
personal *goals* scored **higher** on objective SMART quality yet produced **lower psychological
ownership, commitment and importance**, and at two-week follow-up only **46.6%** of the AI-goal group had
acted on ≥2 goals vs **72.8%** of the self-authored group, with **psychological ownership** (not goal
quality) mediating every downstream effect — and the erosion **steepest for people low in trait
self-efficacy** (the ones most likely to reach for AI help). This is a *second, independent, preregistered
causal* test of the same ownership-erosion mechanism Lee et al. (*Sci Rep* 2026, already anchored) found
for task *execution* — but one level upstream, at goal-*authorship* — so it strengthens the project's
best actionable thread ("how you use AI, not whether, governs its meaning effect") with a distinct
paradigm. The week's second item, folded in Mon Jul 27, is not data but a **new lens**: the Bipartisan
Policy Center's *"Trapped Workers"* network analysis operationalises the project's "does the new-jobs
mechanism hold?" watchlist question as an *empirical mobility-network property* — not "is this job
exposed?" but "is your realistic *next* job also exposed?" Neither item moves the synthesis; both extend
it. The one thing the broader sweep surfaced beyond the dailies is a press narrative worth naming: the
**"AI redundancy washing"** framing (Deutsche Bank; reporting that ~6-in-10 firms admit badging
financially-driven cuts as AI-driven) — no dataset, but it *reinforces* the project's long-running
attribution-skepticism, so it's logged as texture.

## Notable items

- **Chi, Rietsche, Göldi, Ungar & Guntuku, "Optimized but Unowned: How AI-Authored Goals Undermine the
  Motivation They Are Meant to Drive"** — arXiv:2605.12344 (submitted 12 May, rev. 13 May 2026; Univ. of
  Pennsylvania / Bern Univ. of Applied Sciences). Logged in the Jul 21 daily as a **catch-up** (~10 weeks
  old, missed by prior scans; grep-confirmed never previously logged), **primary verified this week
  (arXiv HTML v2 + abstract).** 🟢 **preregistered experiment, with caveats** (N=470; two-week behavioural
  follow-up; preprint — not yet peer-reviewed; single-session goal-setting task, not workplace field data;
  short horizon; US/university-adjacent sample likely). *Shows:* participants given **LLM-authored**
  personal goals (vs self-authored) ended up with objectively *better-formed* goals by SMART criteria
  (d=2.26) but reported **lower psychological ownership** (d=1.38), commitment (d=1.19) and perceived
  importance (d=1.13); at 2-week follow-up, only **46.6%** had acted on ≥2 goals vs **72.8%** of the
  self-authored group. **Mediation:** psychological ownership — *not* objective goal quality — explains
  every downstream motivational/behavioural gap. **Heterogeneity:** people **low in trait self-efficacy**
  (most likely to reach for AI help) showed the **steepest** ownership erosion — a plausible AI-inequality
  channel (those least equipped to compensate lose the most). *Significance for the meaning niche:* a
  **second, independent, preregistered *causal*** test of the mechanism Lee et al. (*Sci Rep* 2026) found
  for task execution — AI authorship of an identity-relevant artifact corrodes ownership and downstream
  motivation *regardless of the artifact's objective quality* — but here applied to **goal-setting
  itself**, one level upstream of task execution. It sharpens the project's "how you use AI, not whether"
  thread: the erosion is not about *doing* the task with AI but about *whose the intention is*. The
  low-self-efficacy heterogeneity is the most notable new wrinkle — it inverts the augmentation-optimist
  hope that AI most helps the least-skilled: on the *ownership/meaning* axis it may cost them most. →
  **added to evidence_base** as a 🟢 anchor (preprint caveats explicit), paired with Lee et al. under "How
  AI is used governs its meaning effect." https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.12344

- **Bipartisan Policy Center, "Trapped Workers: A Network Analysis of Worker Mobility in the AI Economy"**
  — issue brief, 23 Jul 2026 (folded in via the Jul 27 morning scan; verified from primary). 🟡
  (descriptive network/mobility analysis over observed transitions, not causal or experimental; centrist
  think tank, no obvious commercial conflict; model-based exposure classification with self-acknowledged
  limits). *Shows:* using **595,000 observed US worker occupation-to-occupation transitions (2019–2026)**,
  maps 16 occupational mobility clusters and asks not just "is this occupation AI-exposed?" but "where do
  workers actually go next, and is *that* destination also exposed?" — distinguishing **"escapable"**
  (exposed role, but typical next steps are lower-exposure — e.g. medical transcriptionists → lower-exposure
  healthcare roles) from **"trapped"** (typical next steps are similarly exposed — e.g. tax preparers →
  other exposed administrative/accounting/bookkeeping roles). The trapped share is **highly
  scenario-dependent**: **10 of 31** highly-exposed occupations (32%) under a "moderate" (routine-tasks-only)
  scenario vs **58 of 85** (68%) under an "aggressive" (complex-cognitive-work-included) scenario — press
  coverage citing "two in three trapped" quotes *only* the aggressive scenario, not a settled figure.
  *Authors' own limits:* mobility patterns are backward-looking (2019–2026 observed transitions) and don't
  account for new roles AI might create or for augmentation-vs-replacement workflows. *Significance:* a
  genuinely new **methodological lens** on the "does the 'new jobs' mechanism hold?" watchlist question —
  it operationalises "can a displaced worker actually land somewhere" as an **empirical mobility-network
  property** rather than an assumption, complementing the exposure-level literature already in
  evidence_base (Stanford/ADP, ECB, PwC, Census BTOS, IMF). *Not yet a 🟢 anchor* (no causal
  identification, scenario-sensitive headline, single think-tank release). A companion brief exists,
  **"Trapped Workers: Who AI Leaves Behind"** — watch for its full methodology and any independent /
  peer-reviewed replication. → not added to evidence_base; logged as a 🟡 idea to watch.
  https://bipartisanpolicy.org/issue-brief/trapped-workers-a-network-analysis-of-worker-mobility-in-the-ai-economy/

## Running threads

- **"How AI is used governs its meaning effect" (the augmentation/automation axis as psychology) —
  strengthened this week.** Chi et al. gives the ownership-erosion mechanism a **second independent
  preregistered paradigm** and pushes it **upstream** from task-execution (Lee et al.) to
  goal-*authorship*: the corrosive thing isn't doing the work with AI, it's the intention not being
  *yours*. Two distinct experimental designs, same causal direction, same mediator (psychological
  ownership). The new wrinkle worth tracking: the **low-self-efficacy heterogeneity** — the augmentation
  story's "AI helps the least-skilled most" (Brynjolfsson call-center; Anthropic human-capital-enables-use)
  may **reverse on the ownership/meaning axis**. **Track:** any field/workplace (not lab) replication of
  the goal-authorship effect; whether the self-efficacy interaction shows up in Lee-style task paradigms.
- **"Does the new-jobs mechanism hold?" — a first mobility-network instrument.** BPC "Trapped Workers"
  is the first tool the log has that measures the mechanism's *precondition* directly — not "is this job
  exposed?" but "does the displaced worker's realistic next step escape exposure?" It sits alongside the
  exposure-level literature as a **new question shape**, not a new answer (backward-looking transitions;
  no new-role accounting; scenario-sensitive headline). **Track:** the companion "Who AI Leaves Behind"
  methodology; independent/peer-reviewed replication; whether a mobility-network view changes the
  "edges vs middle" read from W29 (are the trapped occupations concentrated at the career edges or the

  aggressive-capability scenario **~two-thirds (58/85) of highly-exposed occupations are "trapped"** (the
