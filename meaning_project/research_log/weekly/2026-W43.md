## The week in one paragraph

**A quiet in-window week whose value came, again, from *catching up* — but this time the catch lands on the project's
central nerve rather than its labour-market edge.** Nothing in-niche was *dated* to Oct 19–25: the macro layer is between
prints (the October jobs report lands Nov 6), the Anthropic Economic Index is unchanged (still Mar-2026 "Learning
curves," Feb-2026 data), the sweep returned no new on-axis UBI *wellbeing* result and no new causal AI-labour paper
carrying a W43 date. What the widened sweep surfaced was a **genuine, previously-unlogged cluster of public-opinion data**
directly on the question the manifesto exists to ask. Elon University's **Imagining the Digital Future Center** (Lee
Rainie, director) ran two national surveys the base had never logged: **"Human Purpose in Peril"** (n=1,505 US adults
incl. 1,163 LLM users; fieldwork May 14–18, 2026; released Sep 14) finds **64%** agree that "by replacing human beings in
most tasks, AI will undermine our sense of purpose and meaning in life" (vs 36% choosing the optimistic framing), **69%**
say AI will weaken human-to-human bonds (a lot 33% / somewhat 36%), **55%** are not confident "their fellow humans will
retain their ability to think critically," and **46%** are not confident "people will maintain their sense of motivation
and purpose as AI advances" — even as the same LLM users report tangible benefits (88% "routinely learn something new and
valuable"; 70% used AI "to create work superior to what they might have achieved on their own"). **"The Meaning of Work in
the AI Age"** (n=1,158 US adults + a 150-worker oversample; fieldwork Aug 20–24, 2026, by SSRS; released Oct 13) is the
more on-niche of the two: **83%** expect AI to eliminate more jobs than it creates, **68%** expect most jobs to pay too
little for a decent lifestyle, **62%** expect most available jobs to be dull and unfulfilling, and **65%** agree that "if
AI makes work less meaningful, people will lose their sense of purpose and dignity" — against a baseline in which **70% of
workers** rate paid work extremely/very important to their *sense of meaning and purpose*, **81%** to their family role,
**64%** to personal identity, and **58%** to community responsibility. The report also finds a thin safety net in public
expectation: only **11%** of workers are highly confident government agencies would help if AI significantly affected
their jobs (**58%** have little/no confidence), **60%** agree workers must adapt themselves rather than expect outside
help, and **59%** agree AI-generated wealth should be widely shared. **Why this is a catch and not a 🟢 meaning datum:**
both are **perception/attitude** surveys — every figure is a self-reported *expectation, belief, or fear*, not a measured
decline in purpose, loneliness, or wellbeing — so they belong on the same shelf as Chueri et al.'s demand-side finding
(the public, asked, wants work's *role* protected, not just its income) and as the exact *mirror* of Anthropic's
"Cadences" survey, where heavy-automation users expected *benefit*; the project's W27 discipline (expectation ≠ outcome,
keep them strictly separate) governs both. What the two ITDF surveys add that the base genuinely lacked is a third layer:
after twenty-odd weeks of *supply-side* labour evidence (ADP, Census, Chandar, BOK, CRR) and *outcome-side* psychology
(the relatedness cluster, Lee et al., Chi et al.), here is the first systematic reading of the **public's own belief**
about AI and meaning — and it is a braced, near-consensus pessimism that the economic–meaning decoupling will go *badly*
(jobs *and* meaning both threatened), which is itself a demand-side fact worth a concept note. Alongside the catch, the
only in-window hard content was a wave of **AI-linked layoff announcements** (Oct 22–23: Barclays ~5,000, Coinbase ~14%,
Block 4,000+, Kroger 3,000 corporate over three years, Snap ~16%, Microsoft buyouts) and a **Goldman Sachs** forecast
(Oct 22) that up to **~9%** of workers could be displaced "during the AI transition" (plus a ~3% hiring drag) — the former
is self-attributed corporate framing and the latter a proprietary projection, both **below the evidence bar** by the
project's standing treatment of Challenger-style announcements, but Goldman's point that AI disruption "is limited when
the economy is healthy and accelerates during cyclical slowdowns" is a clean echo of the **W40** read (a cooling labour
market is exactly where an AI effect could *hide*). Everything else was the discipline of *not* logging.
