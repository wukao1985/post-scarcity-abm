## The week in one paragraph

**A quiet week whose scattered items converged on one message: the AI-labour signal is real, narrow, edge-
concentrated, and — increasingly — hedged by its own sources.** After W33's double durability-test and
W34's late Q3 catch (Zhang et al.), the aggregate machinery stayed quiet: no new jobs report (the August
Employment Situation, the next hard aggregate point, is due ~early September), no new empirical NBER/arXiv
labour paper in-window (only already-anchored or theoretical pieces resurfacing), the AI-cited layoffs
tracker running at its unchanged figure (~365 events / ~209K workers 2026-to-date, methodologies still
inconsistent across trackers), and the recurring SDT-and-AI conference cluster again recapitulating Lee et
al./ILO WP170 without new causal identification (rejected continuously since Jul 20). Into that silence,
four items — and their *coherence* is the week's real content. **(1)** The **Bank of Korea** found youth
(15–29) employment fell 285,000 over Jun-2022→Jun-2026 with **94% of the loss concentrated in high-AI-
exposure sectors**, while workers 50+ *gained* 230,000 jobs (75% in the same sectors) — a near-mirror
generational divergence, the same shape as the US Stanford/ADP + CRR pair, now replicated in Korea's
seniority-wage system, but with the central bank explicitly declining a clean causal claim ("amplifying,"
not "culprit"). **(2)** A **Dixon/YouGov survey** (fielded late Jul, reported 27 Aug) found only ~**3%** of
*currently-employed* US workers self-attribute a past job loss to AI — the mirror-image population to the
laid-off-worker surveys, reporting a much lower self-attributed rate, consistent with "AI risk is
concentrated and narrow, not broadly felt." **(3)** A **SIEPR brief** (McEntarfer — former BLS
Commissioner — et al., 6 Jul, surfaced 30 Aug) complicates the canaries narrative from *inside*:
unemployment since 2022 rose *less* among the most-AI-exposed workers (+0.77pp) than the least-exposed
(+0.85pp). **(4)** An **Apollo (Slok) analysis** (30 Jul, surfaced 30 Aug) reads the effect as showing up in
**wages, not headcount** (AI-exposed wage growth ~6.7pp slower, worst at the bottom quartile). Items 2–4 are
stale or below the anchor bar (unpublished survey; two-month-old syntheses; a conflicted asset-manager
source) and were correctly kept out of the evidence base by the dailies; only the BOK item is folded in, as
a clearly-marked 🟡. But together they are a *convergence*, not a list: the picture that has held since W23
— **aggregate calm + concentrated, contested, edge-located harm** — gained an international replication and
two fresh angles (self-attribution among the still-employed; a nascent wage-compression reading), while the
sources producing the sharpest numbers kept hedging their own causal claims. The rest of the week was, as
usual lately, the discipline of *not* logging: two commercial layoff-perception surveys (Resume Genius,
Resume.org) and two stale loneliness cohort studies correctly refused.

## Notable items

- **Bank of Korea — "Is Youth Employment Contraction Due to AI? Changing Career Ladders and Response Tasks"**
  (청년고용 위축, AI탓인가?) — BOK Issue Note, Employment Research Team, Research Department; released **18 Aug
  2026**; caught in the Aug-29 daily as a **missed-item catch** (grep-confirmed never previously logged;
  slipped past every prior scan). 🟡 **institutional-via-secondary** (read across 8+ consistent Korean and
  international outlets — Korea Times, Korea JoongAng Daily, MLex, Xinhua, Seoul Economic Daily, Tech Times,
  IANS, Seoulz — all attributing the same figures/title/hedge to the same report; **the primary BOK PDF was
  not retrieved directly this session** — flagged for a primary read before any article citation). Method:
  National Pension Service enrollment records merged with the national labour-force survey, individuals
  tracked Jun-2022→Jun-2026, sectors classified by AI-exposure (info/IT services, publishing, computer
  programming, professional services = high-exposure; automation-heavy vs augmentation-heavy split). *Shows:*
  youth (15–29) employment fell **285,000** over the four years, of which **268,000 (94%)** was in high-AI-
  exposure sectors (info services −31.4%, publishing −27.4%, computer programming −16.6%, professional
  services −11.6%); workers **50+ gained 230,000** jobs over the same window, **173,000 (~75%)** in the same
  high-exposure sectors — a near-mirror generational inversion. Youth share of all new hires fell from a
  2014–2019 average of 36.9% to 29.3% in 2024–2026. Automation-heavy industries saw larger youth declines
  than augmentation-heavy ones. **Crucially, the BOK's own framing is hedged, not alarmist:** AI is better
  understood as "accelerating or amplifying pre-existing changes in hiring and work patterns" than as the
  "culprit" — the authors do not claim a clean causal identification. *Significance for the meaning niche:*
  the **first non-US institutional replication** of the young/old AI-exposed employment divergence the
  project anchors via Stanford/ADP (US young-cohort, 19% gap) and CRR (US older-worker mirror) — the same
  edges-vs-middle shape, in a structurally different labour market (Korea's seniority-wage system, national-
  pension data as the source), with the same honest correlational caveat the US findings carry. Strengthens
  the *international generality* of the "AI-exposed career edges fraying" thread without overclaiming
  causation — and the source institution supplying its own causal hedge is itself the (methodologically
  useful) point. → **folded into evidence_base** as a 🟡 international companion to the Stanford/ADP + CRR
  cluster (with the primary-PDF-read flag and the BOK's causal-hedge preserved verbatim). Follow-up: locate
  and read the primary BOK Issue Note PDF directly before any citation.

## Running threads

- **The AI-attribution dispute (young/old edge harm) — the thread that moved, via a first international
  replication.** The most-contested cluster in the project got its **first non-US institutional data point**:
  the BOK Issue Note replicates the young-cohort decline *and* the older-worker mirror in one report, in
  Korea's structurally different labour market, with 94% of youth losses in high-AI-exposure sectors and a
  near-symmetric 50+ gain. This is a genuine strengthening of the "edges fray first" reading — *international
  generality*, not just a US artifact — but it arrives with the same correlational caveat the US findings
  carry, and the BOK's own "amplifying, not culprit" hedge is doing exactly the honest work the project
  prizes. **Track:** locate the primary BOK PDF; watch whether other central banks/statistical agencies
  publish similar age-divergence analyses. The SIEPR complication (most-exposed unemployment rose *less* in
  aggregate) is the counter-texture to hold alongside it — edge-concentration is a *composition* story, not
  an aggregate-unemployment story.
- **A new minor thread — wage-compression (AI in slower pay, not headcount).** Apollo/Slok this week joins
  the existing Jiang et al. "extended workday" rent-capture finding and the IMF "wages without headcount"
  reading to make a recurring shape: AI's near-term bite may fall on *compensation and hours* more than
  *employment counts*. This sits in explicit **tension with the ECB wage-null** (no significant wage impact
  in its reallocation DiD) — so it is a genuine open question, not a settled finding. **Track:** whether a
  peer-reviewed or non-bank institutional source replicates the wage-compression reading; if it holds, it
  matters for the meaning niche because wage erosion without job loss keeps Jahoda's latent functions
  nominally intact while degrading the *extrinsic* rewards and the sense of a fair exchange — a different
  meaning-threat than displacement.

## Watchlist (the analogy breakpoints)

- **What is being automated (muscle → routine cognition → judgment/creativity)?** No frontier-capability
  movement with a distinguishable labour/meaning angle this week. The BOK report touches *where* automation
  bites in the labour data: youth entry-level, routine tasks, in automation-heavy sectors — "AI begins to
  replace routine, entry-level tasks, while senior-level work involves specialised, organisation-specific
  tasks harder to replace" (BOK's own framing). That is the routine-cognition frontier, edge-located, exactly
  as the project reads it. **Movement:** none on capability; one international confirmation of *where in the
  workforce* routine-cognition automation shows up first (the entry rung).
- **Speed vs society's adjustment timescale?** No aggregate pace reading this week (next: August jobs report,
  imminent). The Korean data are a *timescale* note of a kind: a four-year window (2022–2026) already shows a
  285K youth contraction concentrated in exposed sectors — fast enough to appear in national pension records
  within four years — while the institution reading it still declines a clean causal claim. The recurring
  shape holds: **subgroup/edge harm is visible and datable before any aggregate signal, and even then the
  causal attribution stays contested.** **Movement:** none on aggregate pace; a reminder that composition
  shifts outrun aggregate signals and outrun causal certainty.
- **Does 'new jobs appear' still hold (or does AI do the new jobs too)?** No new instrument this week; the
  W30 BPC "Trapped Workers" mobility lens and the Stanford/ADP + CRR reallocation reading remain the state of
  the art. The BOK's generational inversion (youth lose, 50+ gain, *in the same exposed sectors*) is a
  reallocation-*across-cohorts* datum rather than a new-jobs datum — the jobs didn't vanish, they shifted to
  older workers — which is a different mechanism from "new jobs appear" and worth noting as such.
  **Movement:** none on the new-jobs mechanism; a cross-cohort reallocation observation.
