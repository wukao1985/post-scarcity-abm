## The week in one paragraph

**A "who bears the cost" week, with the credentialed-economist consensus visibly catching up.** After
W28 produced *no* new empirical evidence at all, W29's standout is a real 🟢 find: the **Boston College
Center for Retirement Research** brief (Sanzenbacher, Jun 2026, read from the primary PDF) — the first
hard data this log has on **late-career** AI displacement. It shows that before ChatGPT, older workers
(55+) in AI-exposed jobs were *less* likely to leave work than peers (the normal high-skill pattern);
**after** ChatGPT that protective gap flipped, and the rise is specifically in **involuntary
unemployment, not retirement** — displacement, not the "workers voluntarily retiring to dodge AI"
narrative press coverage had been running. Its significance is not the datapoint alone but the *shape*
it completes: paired with the already-anchored Stanford/ADP young-cohort drop, it suggests **AI is
fraying the two edges of a career — entry (22–25) and late (55+) — while the middle holds.** That is a
sharper "who bears the cost" story than either finding alone, and it sharpens the mortality/cognitive
stakes (Kouchekinia: involuntary job loss → ~2 years' cognitive ageing, concentrated in men 51–64 —
the *same* demographic). The week's second real item is not data but **consensus**: the Stanford-
organised **"We Must Act Now"** statement (13 Jul) — 200+ economists and **16 Nobel laureates**,
including **Acemoglu and Johnson, previously vocal AI-displacement skeptics** — warning that AI's *pace*
may outrun institutions. 🟡 institutional texture on the speed-vs-adjustment breakpoint: no new dataset,
but the clearest sign yet that mainstream (not fringe) economists now treat the *pace* of change as the
live risk. Everything else was quiet or recirculated. One honest correction to the dailies: the Jul 14
scan logged Ramp/Revelio as a fresh "catch," but that firm-level study was **already folded into the
W27 digest** (via the Jul 1 Bloomberg synthesis) and lives in concepts/idea_pool as the entry-level
"firm-level counterweight" — the Jul 14 scan's real contribution was locating the **primary source**
(ramp.com/data, 30 Jun) and verifying the figures directly, a *sourcing upgrade*, not a new finding.

## Notable items

- **"Are the Careers of Older Workers Being Cut Short by AI?"** — Geoffrey T. Sanzenbacher, **Center for
  Retirement Research at Boston College**, *Issue in Brief* 26-13 (June 2026; surfaced via CNBC 13 Jul,
  **read directly from the CRR primary PDF**). 🟢 **with caveats** (CPS longitudinal panel 2014–2026,
  N=102,097 observations of workers 55+, matched to occupation-level AI exposure from Tufts' Digital
  Planet Initiative; probit with a pre/post-ChatGPT interaction term and demographic/job controls;
  author's own caveats below). *Shows:* **before** ChatGPT, older workers in more AI-exposed occupations
  were *less* likely to leave work (1-SD exposure → **−0.55pp** exit probability — the normal "high-skill
  jobs last longer" pattern). **After** ChatGPT that gap nearly vanished and reversed: 1-SD exposure →
  **+0.50pp** exit probability, driven specifically by a **+0.28pp rise in unemployment (involuntary job
  loss)** — **not** by retirement or voluntary exit (those bars are near-zero). For computer programmers,
  predicted transitions to non-employment rose 8.7%→11.1% (>25% relative); for painters (low exposure),
  13.5%→13.7% (~2%). A widely-cited secondary summary renders this as "**55+ workers in AI-exposed jobs
  ~25% more likely to leave than before ChatGPT**." *Limits (author's own):* correlational, not a clean
  natural experiment — the author flags that Trump-administration R&D cuts hitting AI-exposed sectors
  could *exaggerate* the estimate while an AI-startup hiring boom could *understate* it; occupation-to-
  Census crosswalk imperfect; single country; data through ~April 2025. *Significance for the meaning
  niche:* the **older-worker mirror** of the Stanford/ADP young-cohort finding — together they point to
  **AI-exposed career *edges* (entry + late) fraying while the middle holds**, a sharper "who bears the
  cost" framing than either alone. It also **corrects an anecdote** the project should have been wary of:
  press framing (WSJ "Workers Opt to Retire Amid Shift to AI") implied *voluntary* retreat, but the hard
  CPS data locate the rise in the **involuntary** channel — displacement, not opt-out. Strengthens the
  already-anchored cognitive/mortality stakes (Kouchekinia: involuntary job loss → ~2 yrs cognitive
  ageing, men 51–64 — the overlapping demographic). → **added to evidence_base** as a 🟢 anchor, paired
  with Stanford/ADP under a "the edges fray first" framing.
  https://crr.bc.edu/are-the-careers-of-older-workers-being-cut-short-by-ai/

- **"We Must Act Now": a Nobel-laureate-backed economist statement on AI's economic transformation** —
  Stanford Digital Economy Lab (organised by Brynjolfsson, Agrawal, Korinek, Cunningham), released **13
  Jul 2026** (wemustactnow.ai; org page + Axios/PR Newswire/Rotman coverage verified). 🟡 (a signed
  expert-consensus policy statement, **not** empirical data — explicitly frames outcomes as *not
  predetermined*, contingent on policy choice, not a prediction). *Shows:* 200+ economists and AI
  researchers, incl. **16 Nobel laureates**, warn AI could bring an economic transformation "larger than
  the Industrial Revolution… arriving much faster," naming **large-scale job displacement** as the
  explicit risk alongside possible living-standard gains, and calling for research + institutions to keep
  AI complementing rather than displacing workers. *Notable:* co-signers include **Daron Acemoglu and
  Simon Johnson** (2024 Nobel), previously among the most prominent *skeptics* of AI-displacement alarm —
  their signing reads as a shift in the credentialed-economist center of gravity, not new data. *Limits:*
  a values/advocacy statement; cites no new dataset; by design takes no position on whether displacement
  *will* happen, only that the stakes and pace warrant preparation now. *Significance:* directly on the
  **speed-vs-adjustment** watchlist variable (ILO J-curve, Mertens/MIT "rising tides" pace) — the
  clearest institutional signal yet that mainstream economists now treat the *pace*, not just the
  eventual scale, as the live risk. Organised by the same lab behind the Stanford/ADP young-cohort
  anchor. Logged as 🟡 institutional texture, not a 🟢 datum. https://digitaleconomy.stanford.edu/news/wemustactnow/

- **Ramp × Revelio Labs, "A New Look at AI's Impact on Jobs" — a *sourcing catch*, not a new finding** —
  Ramp Economics Lab / Revelio Labs, released 30 Jun 2026 (ramp.com/data/ai-jobs-impact). 🟡 (real
  corporate-card AI-vendor *spending* matched to workforce panel data — richer than a survey or exposure
  proxy — **but** Ramp is a commercially interested fintech, and the authors flag selection bias:
  "AI adopters are not a random sample of employers"). *Shows:* firms in the **top third** of AI
  spending-per-employee grew total headcount ~**10.2%** and entry-level headcount ~**12%** over the two
  years after adoption, while the bottom two-thirds saw no significant employment change. *Honest
  reconciliation:* the Jul 14 daily logged this as a fresh "catch," but **it is not new to the project** —
  it was already folded into the **W27 digest** (via the Jul 1 Bloomberg synthesis) and lives in
  concepts.md/idea_pool.md as the entry-level **"firm-level counterweight."** The Jul 14 scan's genuine
  contribution was locating the **primary source** and verifying the figures directly (a sourcing upgrade
  from the Bloomberg wrapper). Given the primary is now verified and this study keeps recurring as the
  live entry-level tension, it gets its own **compact 🟡 line in evidence_base** this week — carrying the
  unit-of-analysis puzzle against Census BTOS, not resolving it.

- **(Broader-sweep catch, dailies missed) "You Shall Not Pass! Where and Why Developers Draw The Line on
  AI Autonomy"** — Choudhuri, Bird, Badea, Gerosa & Sarma (**Microsoft**); arXiv:2607.00533 (submitted 1
  Jul, revised 11 Jul 2026 — in-window). 🟡 (mixed-methods study of **448 professional developers**;
  cognitive-appraisal + work-design framing; **stated preferences**, self-report, single firm, developers
  only — **not** a measured meaning outcome). *Shows:* developers accept AI *lowest* for tasks that are
  **identity-defining, human-facing, or design-oriented**, and higher for high-demand tasks; "preferences
  for AI autonomy reflect how developers cognitively experience their work." *Significance:* a
  **preference-side corroboration** of *meaning-targeted automation* (Ranjit et al.: the agentic/
  satisfying tasks are disproportionately AI-exposed). The tension is the point — workers want to *keep*
  autonomy exactly over the identity/meaning-bearing tasks that Ranjit shows AI is being *pointed at*.
  Texture inside the augmentation/automation-as-psychology thread, not a 🟢 anchor (preferences ≠
  outcomes). Noted, parked as an idea spark. https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.00533

## Running threads

- **NEW thread — "the edges fray first" (who bears the cost).** The week's real synthesis move: CRR's
  older-worker finding (involuntary exits rising for AI-exposed 55+) is the **late-career mirror** of the
  Stanford/ADP young-cohort drop. Read together — young entry (22–25) and older (55+) fraying while the
  mid-career middle holds — this is the sharpest "who bears the AI labour cost" picture the log has, and
  it ties to the Manning & Aguirre "double-jeopardy" cohort (older, clerical/admin, low adaptive
  capacity) and the Kouchekinia cognitive-decline stakes (men 51–64). **Caveat:** all correlational and
  cohort-selective — the "edges vs middle" read could be an artifact of *which* cohorts have been studied
  (young and old have dedicated instruments; the middle is under-measured). **Track:** any mid-career

## Watchlist (the analogy breakpoints)

- **What is being automated (muscle → routine cognition → judgment/creativity)?** No frontier-capability
  movement with a distinguishable labour/meaning angle. The CRR finding gives a small *deployment*
  texture: the sharpest older-worker hit is on **computer programmers** (8.7%→11.1% non-employment
  transitions) — judgment-adjacent cognitive work, consistent with the routine-cognition-reaching-into-
  professional-work creep the project tracks. **Movement:** none measured; one occupational texture point.
- **Speed vs society's adjustment timescale?** No new pace *data* (no jobs report, no new task-level
  study). But "We Must Act Now" *is* an adjustment-timescale argument — 16 Nobel laureates asserting the
  pace may outrun institutions is the project's speed-vs-adjustment breakpoint, now stated by the
  mainstream-economics center. **Movement:** framing-level, on exactly this variable; none on underlying
  pace.
- **Does 'new jobs appear' still hold (or does AI do the new jobs too)?** Mild *negative* texture: CRR
  finds displaced AI-exposed older workers flowing into **unemployment**, not into new roles — a small
  data point that the loop is **not** closing for the displaced at the late-career edge (consistent with
  the ECB wage-null and Goldman's "new jobs are data-centre construction, not re-employed admin workers").
  **Movement:** one negative texture point at the older edge; the firm-level pro-mechanism signal
  (Ramp/Revelio) is unchanged and unreplicated.
