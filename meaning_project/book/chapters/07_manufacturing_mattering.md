# Chapter 7 — Manufacturing Mattering
## 第7章 · 制造被需要

> **状态：🟫 部分成稿。本章主导层是 🔴（设计/价值）。** 标准不是"会发生所以准备"，而是
> "在多种可能未来下都站得住"（robust design）。论证骨架已立，几乎每个具体方案都需研究事项
> 去找真实经验证据，否则只是好听的设计推演。

Chapter 6 was about what *you* can do. This chapter raises the harder question: what can a
*society* do — deliberately, by design — to supply the thing money and markets won't?

Here I have to be honest about the register shift. Everything up to now leaned on evidence about
what *is*. This chapter is mostly about what we should *choose* — it's 🔴, design and values, not
prediction. The honest standard changes accordingly: not "is this true" but **"is this a robust
bet — something worth doing across a wide range of possible futures, even though we can't predict
which one we'll get?"**

### The core idea: mattering as infrastructure

Pull together the book's findings. The hole work leaves (Ch.1) is structure, contact, purpose,
status, competence. Money fills none of it (Ch.2). The most defensible meaning is in
being-needed and being-present (Ch.6). The thread running through all of it is **mattering** —
the sense that one's existence makes a difference to other people.

The proposal — and I'll own it as a proposal — is that *mattering* is the thing a post-work
society would need to deliberately manufacture, the way the 20th century learned to deliberately
manufacture income security through pensions and insurance. Not "give people money" (a floor),
but "build structures in which people are genuinely needed by other people."

What might that concretely mean? Candidates, each 🔴 and each needing real-world evidence before
I'd call it more than a hopeful design:

- **Paid or structured care and community roles** — making the socially valuable, currently
  unpaid or undervalued work of looking after each other into something with structure, status,
  and recognition.
- **Civic and local institutions that need participants** — not make-work, but roles where the
  contribution is real and the absence would be felt.
- **Protecting and resourcing the AI-resistant domains** from Chapter 6 rather than letting them
  be hollowed out by the same efficiency logic.

### Why "robust" is the right test (and the honest one)

I can't tell you AI will definitely cause mass displacement, so I can't justify these on "it's
coming, prepare." The robust-design move is different and more honest: *these are bets that look
good across many futures.* If AI displaces enormous amounts of work — they're essential. If it
mostly augments and the disruption stays mild — building structures where people matter to each
other is still good, just less urgent. There's no future in which "more people feel genuinely
needed" is a bad outcome. That asymmetry — large upside if the disruption is big, little downside
if it isn't — is what makes it a robust bet rather than a gamble on a forecast.

### For you, now

The personal version doesn't wait for policy. 🔴 (my counsel, clearly marked): you can
manufacture your own mattering — deliberately become someone specific people genuinely rely on.
Not for the output you produce, but for your presence and care. The evidence from Chapters 1, 2,
and 6 all point the same way: this is the part of meaning that's both most durable against AI and
most resistant to being bought. I can't prove it's the answer for you. I can tell you it's where
the reasoning in this book honestly leads, and that I'm betting my own life on it being roughly
right.

That bet — acting wisely without certainty — is the subject of the final chapter.
